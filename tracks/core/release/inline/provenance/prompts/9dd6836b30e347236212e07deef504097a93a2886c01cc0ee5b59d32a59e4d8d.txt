At the final rehearsal, Sal sold the same woman a spool of blue thread six times. On the seventh, Evelyn said, “Could you possibly let go of it?”

Sal looked down. They each had two fingers on the spool.

“Sorry,” Sal said.

“Save it up,” called Ruth from the middle of the auditorium. “You’ve only got four lines and that isn’t one.”

This got the laugh Ruth had meant it to get. Sal let go. The spool dropped and rolled under the counter, where the real shopkeeper, if she had any sense, would have left it.

From the wings Jo pushed another spool through the little hatch Sal had built for exactly this contingency. Evelyn took it. The scene went on.

For twenty years Sal had supplied the Bell Street Players with things that could pass through a hatch. They had made roast chickens that would bounce, love letters in six different hands, and a wedding cake with one edible corner. When Ruth announced *The Street of Widows*, Sal had volunteered to play Mrs. Vale, who sold sewing notions. They wanted to stand once inside a room they had made and have somebody come in the door.

“Saturday night and Sunday afternoon,” Ruth had promised. “You’ll do both.”

Jo, seventeen, was the understudy. She had come in originally to record the building. She wanted to make sound for radio plays, an occupation Sal had believed went out with their father’s teeth in a glass. For three weeks she had been going around in headphones, tapping radiators. She had a recording of the ticket window going up that she claimed sounded precisely like a guillotine. Sal wondered about her sources.

They had practiced Mrs. Vale’s scene while laying out its properties. Scissors closed, handles downstage. Three cards of buttons, red on top. The spool on its side so Evelyn, whose hands shook, could scoop it up. Jo played both parts until Sal learned to hear the difference between a silence Evelyn meant to leave and one that needed filling.

After rehearsal Jo came through the shop door carrying her recorder. “I like how you do the last line.”

“Because then we’re done?”

“Because you turn away. Like you’d be embarrassed to see if it helped.”

Sal began shutting the drawers. Each had its contents drawn in pencil on the bottom so anybody striking the set could tell what was missing. It had never occurred to them to draw what went on top of the counter. Somebody had put the scissors in a coffee mug again.

“My aunt and my grandpa are coming Sunday,” Jo said. “She traded a shift so she could drive him. It’s the only one.”

Sal took the scissors out of the mug.

“I told them what an understudy is,” Jo added.

“Have they ever seen you act?”

“My grandpa saw me be a flood in fourth grade. It was mostly fabric.”

At home Sal stood at the sink and said the four lines without moving their lips. In twenty years nobody had come to see a chicken.

Saturday at half hour they signed in on the clipboard by the stage door, then went from habit to check the property tables. Jo had already set them. The honey jar held cold tea. The sugar jar held sugar, following a celebrated dispute about what else an actor might agree to put in his mouth. Under the work light the gunmetal scissors lay precisely within their white tape outline.

Sal sat while Jo did up the tiny boot buttons with a bent crochet hook. Out front the audience made the low continuous noise that, heard for twenty years through a wall, had always sounded friendly. Tonight it sounded like people who had bought something.

“Want to run it?” Jo said.

“I’d rather hear about the flood.”

“We drowned three farmers and settled into a lake.”

Then the house lights went down.

Sal spent the first act in the passage with a shawl over their knees. When the shop rolled into place, they helped set the brake until the stage manager slapped their hand very softly. They went round to the front, stood behind the counter, and felt a draft come up through the little hatch.

Evelyn entered in a green coat with the collar gone shiny.

“Morning, Annie,” Sal said.

Nobody laughed. They had somehow expected somebody to laugh at the fact of them speaking. Evelyn asked for the blue thread. Her boy was coming home and his good jacket needed hemming.

“That one’s French,” Sal said.

Evelyn asked how much.

“For you, a penny.”

The spool went neatly from hand to hand. Out on the street the telegram boy passed the window on his bicycle. Everybody in the theater knew where he was going, except the woman in the green coat.

“I’ll put it on your bill,” Sal said, and turned to the drawers.

In the dressing room afterward they found they could recall the whole scene with an almost physical delight, particularly the second or two before Evelyn entered, when the shop had been theirs to keep.

“I want Jo to do it today,” they said. Saying it took some of the pleasure out of having decided it.

“I know. I sold her the tickets.”

At noon Jo was beneath the shop counter, listening to something. Sal sat on the little stool and waited until her headphones emerged.

Jo had been making a three-minute piece for a summer radio workshop. Sal had assumed all her knocking and tapping would eventually go into separate files labeled with names like DOOR. In fact she was telling the play from the telegram boy’s point of view. He had no lines at all.

“Does it end at the house where he delivers it?” Sal asked.

“He has to take the bike back. I think that’s probably the worst part.”

This time they turned away because Annie had begun to weep and a woman ought to be able to get herself out of a shop with her purchase before anyone saw.

In the blackout Sal stood still until the brake came off and the shop began its backward journey with them aboard. Down below, two red lights on Jo’s recorder glowed in the dark. The telegram boy walked his bicycle back along the boards. For the first time Sal heard the loose link in the chain. It clicked once with every turn, at longer and longer intervals as he slowed to set the bicycle against the wall. Jo kept recording through the silence afterward.

For the curtain call they lined up by height in the passage. Sal could feel the hollow the crochet hook had left against their ankle. Across from them Jo was already gathering the things that might go missing, the scissors first, then the two blue spools, one warm from Evelyn’s hand.

The curtain lifted. Sal went out with the boy who rode the bicycle. He had been told to take their hand and he did, formally, as if helping them across a patch of ice. In the third row the man with the folded program was applauding. His niece leaned over to tell him something, and he nodded, still clapping, his face turned toward the strip of darkness beside the shop where Jo stood listening.

Sal bowed. As they straightened, the boy squeezed their fingers in his excitement, and they squeezed back.

### On Account

At the final rehearsal, Sal came through the shop door carrying the shop door’s spare hinge.

“Put that down,” called Miriam from the sixth row.

Sal put it on the counter.

“You’re a shopkeeper. Where would you keep a hinge?”

“Depends who I thought might buy one.”

A few people laughed in the dark. Sal slipped the hinge into their apron pocket and started the entrance again. This time they remembered to wipe their hands before they spoke. The apron was clean. They’d asked Denny from costumes if they could dirty it a little, and he’d given them a look and gone back to pressing the mayor’s trousers.

For twenty years Sal had built things for the Bellwether Players. They’d made a coffin that could take the weight of two grieving men, a wedding cake that came apart at the touch of a fork, and this year’s shop counter, which had to survive being sat on by a woman in wet clothes. Until this autumn the only time Sal had crossed the stage with the lights up was to fetch a false moustache somebody had sneezed into the orchestra pit. When Miriam passed around the audition sheet for *The Diversion*, Sal wrote their name beside SHOPKEEPER before they could think of anyone better suited.

The shopkeeper had four lines. The woman in wet clothes had just walked home along the old railway bed, where the town council had diverted the creek. She meant to buy a pair of socks and charge them to her husband’s account, although her husband had been dead since Act One.

“Cash or account?” Sal said.

“Account. Under Pritchard.”

“We closed that account in March.”

“He always said you were quick with a pencil.”

“You can pay me Thursday.”

“Why Thursday?”

“I’m closed Wednesday.”

Miriam let the blackout go all the way down before she spoke. “That’ll do. Sal, leave your apron on the hook. Denny’s put up a sign.”

The sign said LEAVE APRON ON HOOK. Under it was a second sign in smaller letters that said THIS HOOK.

Jo was in the wings with their school backpack hugged against their chest. They were sixteen and had been reading Mrs. Pritchard for Sal before rehearsals, in the narrow passage between the boiler and the downstairs toilets. Jo read her as more tired than angry. Sometimes, in rehearsal, Sal found themself waiting for Jo’s answer and got a small shock when Ruth gave hers.

“My aunt got the Sunday off,” Jo said. “She’s bringing my granddad.”

Sal hung up the apron. The hinge knocked against the wall.

“I told them I was understudy,” Jo added. “I think Granddad heard the first half of that word and figured it was a scholarship.”

“You want me to talk to Miriam?”

Jo looked up. “Would you?”

Sal had asked the question the way they asked whether someone needed a hand carrying something. It wasn’t until Jo answered that they felt the weight of it.

At the folding table in the lobby, Miriam was counting programs into stacks of fifty. Her husband had printed them at the feed store after closing. For a minute she kept counting, touching each corner with a damp thumb.

“I promised you Saturday and Sunday,” she said at last.

“I know.”

“Do you want Sunday?”

“Yes,” Sal said. It was a relief to hear how quickly it came out.

“Then I’ll tell Jo.”

“I can tell Jo.”

Miriam pulled a program from the stack and handed it over. Sal’s name appeared twice, once opposite SHOPKEEPER and once at the bottom of a long paragraph that began with the loan of a steam iron. They’d been in that paragraph for years. At home they put the program on the kitchen table and ate toast over it, moving it aside when they got to the buttered edge.

On Saturday, Jo arrived early to set the socks. They checked that the paper band would tear when Ruth pulled it, then lined the packet up with the pencil mark inside the drawer. Sal had shown them how to do that. Jo had shown Sal that if you drew the drawer all the way out at once, the stop made a sound like a judge’s gavel.

Sal told them about Sunday while they were both crouched behind the counter.

“Okay,” Jo said. They slid the drawer shut. An instant later they opened it to check the socks again.

“What’ll your folks do?”

“Come anyway. I bought the tickets.” Jo stood and brushed the knees of their jeans. “Granddad says the creek used to come up through the floor at the Rex. He says you could hear it under the picture. I want to get him to tell it in the building before they put the pharmacy in.”

“What for?”

“I want to make a piece you listen to while you walk down Main Street. You get to the laundromat and hear the bowling alley that was there. Stuff like that.” Jo picked a thread off Sal’s apron. “He tells it better when he can point.”

Sal had seen Jo in the empty auditorium holding a little recorder toward the ceiling. They’d assumed it was for school. “Is that why you wanted the sound of the drawer?”

Jo nodded. “I got three good ones. Can I have the door too?”

“After the show. It sticks when the room warms up.”

At places, Sal stood with their thumb on the shop latch and listened to the audience settle. There was a cough from the front row that had the careful, irritated sound of somebody trying to cough only once. Ruth came off from the creek scene and stepped into a shallow baking tray so her wet shoes wouldn’t leave tracks in the wings. Jo took the tray away by both handles. The stage manager raised two fingers and lowered one.

Sal opened the door. At the counter they reached for the pencil and found that their hand shook. They used it anyway, running the point down the ruled page of the ledger. They’d filled the first spread with names from the phone book.

“We closed that account in March.”

Ruth kept one hand in her coat pocket. In rehearsal she’d always taken it out by then. Sal could see the shape of her fingers through the wet cloth and understood, with a certainty that lasted only as long as the scene, that she had come with money and would sooner walk home wet than count it out in front of them.

“You can pay me Thursday,” Sal said.

The laugh at the end was bigger than it had been in rehearsal. It took Sal a moment in the dark to let go of the pencil.

After the show, Jo set the recorder on the counter and had Sal open the door five times. On the fourth try they stopped Sal halfway through and asked them to wait for the refrigerator in the lobby to cut out. In the silence, Sal thought of the next afternoon and felt keenly how much they wanted to open that door again with a roomful of people on the other side.

“Do you still want to do Sunday?” they asked.

Jo took their hands off the recorder. “Yes.”

“I wanted both.”

“I know.”

Sal looked at the counter. Where Ruth sat, the hem of her coat had left a dark stripe on the wood. “I’ll ask Miriam to give you tomorrow. I want her to say your name before the show so your granddad hears it.”

Jo opened their mouth, then looked down. The red light on the recorder was still on. They stopped it. “Would you run it with me once before?”

“Twice,” Sal said. “The first time you’ll go too fast.”

On Sunday, Sal played Mrs. Pritchard in the boiler passage. Jo did go too fast the first time. The second time Sal forgot to say “Why Thursday?” and for a moment they both stood there waiting for the other to speak. Then the boiler came on and they had to put their heads close together to finish.

Miriam made the announcement from the house floor. From the wings Sal could see Jo’s aunt lean over to repeat it into the old man’s ear. He set his program flat on his knees and turned his face toward the shop.

The door stuck. Sal took half a step before Jo leaned their hip into it and got it open. Jo left the ledger shut. When they said, “We closed that account in March,” they looked straight at Ruth, as though the two of them had stood in that room together in March and could have said exactly what the weather was. Ruth sat down on the counter harder than usual. From where Sal stood, they heard the braces take her weight.

At the curtain call Jo came out with the station porter and the woman who played both council clerks. Sal stood just beyond the edge of the light with the shop key in their palm. Jo had left it in the door, where somebody would catch a sleeve on it during the strike.

The old man clapped with the program still in one hand, making a thin slapping sound. Jo found him a little late. They had already bowed once, and the porter was reaching for their hand to take them forward with the rest of the cast. Jo took it. For the second bow they kept their head up until the last possible moment. Sal stayed where they could see Jo’s face.
