**The Good Side**

Nessa learned that her niece meant to sell fabric by the meter when she put her hand under their table for the cash tin and cut her thumb on a yardstick.

“Meter stick,” Ada said, fetching a plaster. “A yardstick wouldn’t do me much good.”

“You’ve sharpened it, anyway.”

The brass end had come loose. While Ada wound the plaster round her thumb, Nessa saw three bolts of cloth standing behind the stall and a fourth already laid across the table. Tucked into the fourth was a card in her niece’s square lettering. *At noon, a wrap skirt cut to fit. Volunteers welcome.*

It was eight on the morning of Gannet Market’s fortieth anniversary. Nessa had sold mended clothes at this spot for twenty of those years. She had been expecting a piece of cake.

“I told you I was bringing the shop ends,” Ada said.

“You did. You said you wanted to see how they went.”

“How did you think I’d find out?”

Nessa’s answer would have been that there were six perfectly good days between Saturdays in which to find things out. But this had been her brother’s table, an oak door on folding legs, left to his wife and daughter and, through an arrangement nobody had ever thought worth writing down, to his sister every Saturday. Ada had spent most Saturdays of her childhood underneath it, sorting buttons for wages she ate in the form of sugared almonds. She had some standing here.

Nessa hung the charcoal jackets along the back rail. She put the yellow dress repaired under both arms where a person coming from the fish stall would see it first. Then she opened the fitting screen.

Ada watched the little square of floor disappear inside it.

“I need that at twelve,” she said.

“So I gathered.”

By nine a wind had got under the anniversary bunting. At the cheese stall opposite, Phil secured his end to a round of aged ewe’s milk, which traveled slowly toward the edge of its board until a customer caught it. At nine fifteen Ada sold two meters of blue cotton to a man who made cages for racing pigeons. He wanted covers to keep the birds quiet on the train.

“Will this shrink?”

“Yes,” Ada said. “How wide are your cages?”

They worked it out. He took another thirty centimeters. Nessa, who would have told him everything shrank sooner or later, admired the precision of the sale.

Her own first customer was Mrs. Palma, come for the black trousers Nessa had shortened and made roomier through the seat. She went behind the screen with her stick. It took some time. Ada’s next customer rested a bag of potatoes on the cutting end of the table. Phil came over for his anniversary raffle tickets, which had been entrusted to Nessa because she possessed a bulldog clip. From inside the screen Mrs. Palma said, “Can everybody hear me pulling my trousers up?”

“Only those who paid extra,” Nessa said.

Ada took the potatoes off the table and gave the woman a stool. Then she stood sideways across the gap where the screen’s third hinge sagged.

Mrs. Palma emerged wearing the trousers and folded her old pair into her shopping bag. “I’d begun to think it was me that was crooked,” she said, and paid the remaining eight pounds.

When the aisle cleared, Ada bent to inspect the hinge.

“Leave it,” Nessa said, more sharply than she meant. “You’ll have the whole thing down.”

“I was seeing which screwdriver.”

On the other side of the aisle Phil had started telling somebody that Nessa was retiring. By ten thirty she had received a bunch of parsley and a kiss on each cheek from the fishmonger, who had known her since she wore her hair in two unfortunate ropes. She went across to Phil.

“I said you were bringing on the next generation,” he protested.

“You said you’d miss me.”

“I will. When you go.”

“Give me six eggs out of your fancy raffle hamper and I’ll overlook it.”

“They’re china.”

“Well, that’s a shocking thing to win.”

When she came back, Ada was lifting the yellow dress off the table so she could unroll the gray linen. Nessa took it from her.

“Where do you see me in this arrangement?” she asked.

“I see you spreading things out as fast as I clear them.”

“I have thirty pounds in that dress, counting my time.”

“I have four hundred and sixty in this cloth, without counting mine.”

That brought them both to a halt. Ada’s sewing shop had closed in March. Nessa knew its owner had moved to be near her grandchildren. She knew less about what had happened to the women who used to work there. She had thought the bolts were remnants Ada had been allowed to take.

“You bought them?”

“At a good price. I can do the school run, I can cut orders at home. But people need to feel it first. Would you buy gray linen off a photograph?”

“No,” Nessa admitted. There were a dozen ways for linen to be unpleasant.

“I can’t get a stall in my name till February. I did tell you about the demonstration.”

“You sent me a picture of a skirt.”

“With words underneath.”

“I thought they were instructions for my making one.”

“Auntie, when have you ever made anything I sent you instructions for?”

At a quarter to twelve the yellow dress found its woman. She came every anniversary with her husband, who bought the same small pork pie and said, every year, that the pies had got smaller. Today he was carrying two. His wife’s name was Linnet. She had a wedding to attend in a registry office with a green carpet and had ruled out several colors on that account.

Nessa zipped her up behind the screen. The dress sat beautifully through the shoulders. At the waist it wanted half an inch on either side, and one pocket bag had worked forward into a soft little ear.

“Could you pin it so I can see?” Linnet asked.

Outside, Ada was explaining how to find the straight grain. The chapel clock began its four preliminary clunks.

“I’ll come back,” Linnet whispered. She began reaching for the zip.

“You’re in it now,” Nessa said.

The clock struck. Nessa knelt with the pins between her lips, aware of the people gathering beyond the thin brown panels. She heard Ada ask for a volunteer, and Linnet’s husband say something that got a laugh.

“Stand the way you ordinarily do,” Nessa told Linnet. “You want to be able to eat the dinner.”

When she drew the screen aside, Linnet’s husband was standing on a flattened potato sack in his shirt and corduroys. Ada had wound a length of striped cotton around his waist. His two pies sat safely on the cheese board opposite. Ada was showing the six or seven people gathered there how a stripe that looked quite vertical on a table could travel a surprising distance over a hip.

“I’d wear that,” Linnet said.

“Get your own,” said her husband.

He turned to admire his backside in Nessa’s mirror. Behind him an elderly woman, trapped with her trolley between the onlookers and the olive stall, said she had seen all she cared to and would somebody let her through.

They moved. The trolley caught the third panel of the screen. Its bad hinge gave with a noise like somebody cracking a knuckle. Nessa caught the panel and held it against her chest while Ada unwound the volunteer. For one instant Nessa was looking straight at the faded paper on the inside of the panel. A child had penciled in a row of buttons, each with its four holes, down the full length of it. She had spent twenty years on the other side.

By two the skirts had sold Ada seven cuts of cotton. Linnet had paid a deposit on the dress and gone home with the promise of pockets that lay flat. The detached panel rested under the table. Two panels made a corner where customers could inspect a collar or a shoulder, and three people who wanted to try trousers went away.

At four Ada fetched a screwdriver from the watch repairer. Nessa counted her cash. After the pitch fee and what she owed the laundry, she had made fifty-one pounds. There had been Saturdays she would have kissed that money. There had been Saturdays she made it by breakfast.

“Every other week?” Ada said, easing the bent screws out.

Nessa shook her head. “People come back for things. You heard me tell Linnet next Saturday.”

“Then I get the morning and you get the afternoon.”

“Who’s going to buy cloth at eight in the morning?”

“The pigeon man.”

They considered him.

“Listen,” Ada said. “I can sell samples off a rail, same as your dresses. All I need the whole table for is cutting.”

At closing they tried it. They hung sample lengths on three skirt hangers. Ada put the bolts across the trestle braces below. Nessa moved her cash tin and folded a stack of jerseys into a wooden apple box. That left enough clear oak to measure on if they turned the cloth and worked along the short edge. Ada cut a meter of blue. It came off square.

“Next Saturday we’ll do it this way,” Nessa said. “Screen’s for fittings. If you’re doing another skirt, give me a time and I’ll book around it.”

“Half past twelve. And put me down first for a fitting, before we open. I want those charcoal trousers if they’ll go over my thighs.”

“They will,” Nessa said. “I’ve had my eye on your thighs all day.”

They put the third panel back with fresh screws. Before folding the screen, Ada ran her finger down the penciled buttons. “I could never get you to sew those on,” she said.

From across the aisle Phil brought two slices of anniversary cake on a paper plate. The icing had stuck to his thumb. They ate sitting on the edge of their table, with one leg each swinging through the space where, next Saturday, the cloth would go.
