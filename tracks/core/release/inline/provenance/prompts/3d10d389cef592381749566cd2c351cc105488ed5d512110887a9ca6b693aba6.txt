# The Spin Cycle Library

The library began because Gus got stuck on page eighty-six.

He was waiting for the dryer to finish with his school uniform, and page eighty-seven of *The Lizard Who Knew Too Much* had been torn out by some criminal. He read page eighty-eight. The lizard was in a canoe. On page eighty-six, the lizard had been in jail.

“How did he get a canoe?” Gus said.

His twin, Bea, looked up from folding a fitted sheet into a shape she called the Meatball. “Ask somebody upstairs. Everybody in this building has books.”

“I know exactly three people upstairs.”

“Then we should meet the rest.”

Bea had lived at 19 Juniper Street for eleven years and still knew most of her neighbors by the sounds they made. There was the Heel Walker, the Midnight Blender, and somebody who sneezed in sets of four. She wanted to know their names. She also wanted someone besides Gus to hear her joke about the Meatball.

Gus wanted enough books in the laundry room to survive a whole wash and dry. He also wanted to find out how a lizard could paddle with those tiny arms.

Ms. Pike, the building manager, considered their proposal while eating a pear over a wastebasket.

“One shelf,” she said. “Two weeks. I’ll come down Saturday at ten to see how it’s going. Keep the folding table clear. People need somewhere to fold.”

Bea glanced at the Meatball under her arm. “So I hear.”

They started with six books from home and a stack of handwritten borrowing cards. On each card, Bea drew a tiny washing machine with feet. Gus made a sign that said PLEASE RETURN BOOKS IN TWO WEEKS. Bea added OR SOONER IF THE ENDING IS GOOD AND YOU WANT TO TALK ABOUT IT.

Their first donor was Mrs. D’Souza from 5B, who arrived carrying a grocery bag against her hip.

“Eight cookbooks,” she said. “My sister sends me one every Christmas. She has a very steady opinion of me.”

All eight were called *Dinner in a Dash*. The edition with a green border promised fast meals for busy families. The edition with a blue border promised quick meals for busy families. Each cover featured the same chicken, although on one it had been turned slightly to the left.

Gus opened three copies to page forty-two. “Same potatoes.”

“Good,” said Mrs. D’Souza. “If there’s a rush on potatoes, you’re ready.”

Bea asked which recipe she liked best.

“Oh, page nineteen. I make it whenever I’m mad at my sister. You get to hit the chicken with a pan.”

She stayed long enough to show them the recipe. By the time she left, Bea knew her first name was Anita and that she was the Midnight Blender. She made mango smoothies after her late shift at the hospital.

Gus moved the cookbooks to one end of the shelf. They occupied most of it. He turned the chicken with the green border to face the wall.

On Tuesday, Mr. Chen from 2A brought down a mystery with a lighthouse on the cover. Gus was halfway through the first chapter when Mr. Chen pointed to the twins’ favorite comic, *Captain Pigeon and the Municipal Menace*.

“Ooh,” he said. “Can I borrow that?”

Gus had put the comic on the shelf himself. He’d liked how it looked there. He’d imagined people saying, “Excellent comic,” and leaving it exactly where it was.

“Sure,” said Bea.

Mr. Chen reached for it. Gus reached for it at the same time. They stood for a moment with one hand each on Captain Pigeon.

“I can bring it back Thursday,” said Mr. Chen.

Gus let go. “The staples are a little loose. And the page with the bus crash folds out. You have to open it like you’re opening a very old sandwich.”

Mr. Chen demonstrated on an imaginary sandwich. His technique was excellent.

Bea wrote his name on a borrowing card and put it on top of the washing machine. The machine went into its spin cycle. The card shivered across the enamel and vanished down the back.

“Well,” said Mr. Chen. “According to your records, I was never here.”

By Friday of the first week, five more cards had followed his. The twins tried weighing the stack down with a bottle of detergent, but the bottle belonged to the Heel Walker, who took it upstairs with her. Her name was Tamika. She wore clogs because she could step out of them without putting down her baby.

“You got anything I can read with one hand?” she asked.

Gus offered her a skinny book of riddles. Tamika read the first one to her baby, who pulled off one sock and ate it.

“He’s thinking,” she said.

Mr. Chen came back on Thursday as promised. The comic was inside a clean cereal box he’d cut into a folder.

“The bus crash was terrific,” he said. “I used to drive the eleven. That pigeon took the corner too fast.”

Gus opened the foldout. Mr. Chen showed him where the captain should have started braking. They went through the whole chase that way, including the part where a delivery truck full of watermelons went down the subway stairs.

“Nothing you can do about that one,” Mr. Chen said.

The next time Gus lent the comic, he sent it out in the cereal box.

On the second Friday, the shelf held eight cookbooks, two mysteries, one sock, and a book about a horse that a kid named Zuri had brought downstairs to trade. The laundry room was busy. Zuri wanted the lighthouse mystery. Tamika wanted another riddle book. Mrs. D’Souza had come down to see whether anybody had tried her chicken. Bea was on the floor with a ruler, trying to fish out the borrowing cards.

She caught a dryer sheet, a button, and something that had once been a pretzel. Then the machine began to spin. The ruler knocked against the wall so fast it sounded like somebody applauding her mistakes.

Gus looked at the shelf. “We could pack it up tonight.”

Bea sat back on her heels. “You want to close?”

“I want to read. I’ve spent forty minutes trying to figure out who has the book about the kid who lives in a museum.”

“Zuri’s dad,” said Zuri.

Gus stared at her.

“He reads it standing up in the kitchen,” she said. “He says if he sits down, somebody will ask him to do something.”

Bea put the ruler away. Across the room, Mr. Chen was showing Tamika how to fold a fitted sheet. Tamika’s baby was wearing the other sock on his hand. Bea had spent two weeks learning people’s names. Now she could hear them using each other’s.

“What would make you want to keep it?” she asked Gus.

“Room for books to come in,” he said. “And I’d like to sit down with one sometime.”

Bea climbed onto the empty laundry cart. “Does anybody want a cookbook to keep?”

Mrs. D’Souza raised her hand. “I want to keep the one with my notes. See if there’s a little drawing of my sister getting hit with a pan.”

There was. Zuri picked a copy with a chicken facing left. Mr. Chen took one because the potatoes looked good, and Tamika took one because she could prop it open and cook with the hand her baby had left her. Mrs. D’Souza offered to take three to the break room at work. That left one for the library. She wrote *USE HALF THE SALT* next to the potatoes before Gus put it back.

Bea found a big binder clip in her school bag. Gus clipped the cards together and slid them inside the open end of the cereal box, which stood upright between the books. They’d still have to look behind the washer for the old cards. Or they could ask Zuri, who turned out to know where all the books were, including one the twins had forgotten they owned.

By nine that night, they’d decided to keep the library. Gus slid the comic into its folder beside the cards. Bea put up a fresh sign with the library’s hours. It said WHENEVER YOU’RE DOWN HERE.

At ten the next morning, Ms. Pike found the folding table clear, nine books on the shelf, and Gus reading in a plastic chair. Bea was sitting cross-legged beside him, listening to Mrs. D’Souza describe the results of her first attempt at the potatoes. Too much salt, even after halving it. She had circled the recipe and written *JUST EAT A POTATO*.

Ms. Pike examined the shelf. Then she tugged on a card. The whole stack came out in her hand.

“Can I borrow this one?” she asked, taking down the book about the horse. “I had it when I was ten.”

Bea handed her a pencil.

As Ms. Pike filled in her name, Gus turned a page. Mr. Chen had brought him another copy of *The Lizard Who Knew Too Much*. Page eighty-seven was intact.

“The canoe was in the jail the whole time,” Gus said.

“Why?” said Bea.

Gus read on. “The sheriff was a duck.”

From the hallway, Ms. Pike called back, “I’m going to need that one next.”

On the first Saturday in March, Inez found her twin brother sitting in the laundry room, reading the instructions on a jug of bleach.

“Interesting?” she asked.

“I’m at the part where somebody calls a doctor,” said Milo.

They had forty-one minutes left on the dryer. Inez looked at the empty shelf above the folding table. Milo looked at the comic sticking out of her coat pocket. By the time their socks were dry, they had invented a library.

Mrs. Bell, the building manager, listened to their proposal while trying to fit a new wheel onto a grocery cart. “You can have that one shelf for two weeks,” she said. “Keep the folding table clear. People get ugly when they have to fold fitted sheets in the air.”

“What happens after two weeks?” Inez asked.

“I come down and see how it’s going.”

Milo held the wheel steady while Mrs. Bell tightened the bolt. “Have you ever seen anybody fold a fitted sheet successfully?”

“Once,” she said. “I still think about her.”

Upstairs, the twins piled possible books on their bedroom floor. Inez wanted the library because the school librarian had said she was too young to work the circulation desk. Apparently a person could be trusted to evacuate calmly in a fire but couldn’t be trusted with a date stamp. She planned to go back with experience.

Milo wanted a place where people would talk to him. They’d lived in the building since September, and most of his conversations with neighbors went like this.

“Holding the elevator?”

“Yep.”

Occasionally somebody said, “Thanks.” Those were his regulars.

They chose a mystery, a book about deep-sea animals, three paperbacks about a horse named Butterscotch, and their favorite comic, *Captain Kidney Bean and the Moon of Doom*. They had read it together so often that either twin could whisper “Initiate chili sequence” and make the other shoot milk out of their nose.

Inez cut borrowing cards from an empty cereal box. Each had spaces for the title, the borrower, and the date. Milo made a sign that said LAUNDRY LIBRARY and drew a washing machine reading in the bathtub.

On opening day, their first visitor was Mr. Vale from upstairs. He arrived hugging eight books to his chest.

“My contribution,” he said.

The first book was *Supper in One Pot*. The second was *More Supper in One Pot*. The third was *Even More Supper in One Pot*. After that the publisher had apparently run out of confidence and started using years.

“My sister sends me one every birthday,” Mr. Vale explained. “Please take them before I turn sixty-nine.”

The eight cookbooks occupied two thirds of the shelf. In their jacket photos the same cheerful woman held the same red pot. Only her glasses changed. By 2007 they were enormous.

“Which one do you recommend?” Milo asked.

Mr. Vale considered. “In 2004 she drops the pot. You can see a little dent in every picture after that.”

He showed Milo. Milo showed Inez. Before long all three of them had their noses pressed against a photograph of braised cabbage. It was the longest conversation Milo had ever had with an upstairs person.

Their second visitor was Ada from 2C, who was nine and had recently cut her own bangs. They sloped downhill toward her left ear.

“Can I borrow *Captain Kidney Bean*?” she asked.

Milo felt an unexpected squeeze in his stomach. He had imagined people admiring their comic. Somehow he had skipped the part where it left the building in somebody’s backpack.

“Page seventeen folds out,” he said. “You have to open it from the top.”

“Okay.”

“And the last page looks blank but there’s a tiny joke in the corner.”

“Are you going to tell me the whole book while I stand here?”

Inez silently handed him a borrowing card. He filled it out. Ada left with the comic, and Milo set the card beside the others on top of the washer.

The washer entered its spin cycle. The card trembled, turned gently clockwise, and slid behind the machine.

All three blank cards followed it.

Inez crouched and peered into the gap. “I can see them. Also a spoon.”

“Can you reach?”

“If we know anybody with a six-foot elbow.”

On Tuesday they tried weighing the cards down with a mitten. A man from the fifth floor borrowed the deep-sea book, recognized his mitten, and took it home. The washer ate the card.

On Wednesday, Mrs. Santiago came in with her little boy, Nico, who was carrying a plastic stegosaurus by the tail. She took *More Supper in One Pot* off the shelf.

“I need something for dinner that Nico will eat.”

“I eat green things,” Nico announced.

“Name one.”

He held up the stegosaurus.

Mrs. Santiago found a recipe for potato pancakes. Milo asked if she could make them in one pot.

“Of course,” she said. “If your pot is a frying pan and somebody comes over afterward to wash the grater and two bowls.”

She borrowed the book. Inez tucked the card under the edge of the sign. Two loads of towels later, it joined its family behind the washer.

By the second Thursday the shelf looked almost exactly as it had on the first Saturday. Two horses had traded places. Somebody had returned the deep-sea book with a drawing of an anglerfish inside. All eight red pots smiled out at the room again.

“We have no records,” Inez said. “If Mrs. Bell asks what’s been borrowed, our official answer is ‘Ask the spoon.’”

Milo was watching the laundry-room door. Ada hadn’t come back yet. He missed Captain Kidney Bean. He especially missed the page where the captain tries to pass himself off as an ordinary burrito at a formal dinner.

“Maybe our good books should stay here,” he said. “People could read them while their stuff dries.”

“Which books are our good books?”

“The ones we’d hate to lose.”

Inez looked at the shelf. “Then the whole lending library would be books we don’t care if anybody brings back.”

They sat on the floor between two baskets of wet laundry. Upstairs, somebody was practicing the same four notes on a trumpet. The fourth one sounded like a screen door opening.

“I know why Ada hasn’t brought it back,” Milo said at last. “She liked it and wants to keep it.”

“You liked it and wanted to keep it,” Inez said. “You lent it to her anyway.”

This was fairly annoying of Inez, since it gave him a better opinion of both himself and Ada when he was in the mood to have a worse one.

Friday evening, they knocked on 2C. Ada answered wearing a colander on her head.

“Your comic’s okay,” she said immediately. “Grandpa reads me six pages after dinner. We do voices. He has to take his teeth out for the Moon King.”

From inside the apartment a voice boomed, “WHO DARES DISTURB MY LUNAR NAP?”

“He can keep his teeth in for that one,” Ada said.

“Keep it till you finish,” Milo said. “Would you write what you just told us on a card?”

Saturday morning, one hour before inspection, the twins took everything off the shelf.

Inez had spent half the night thinking about the people at school who lined up at the circulation desk. Most of them didn’t need help stamping a date. They needed somebody who could say, *Oh, you’d like this one. There’s a dog who inherits a hotel.*

Milo had spent half the night drawing the Moon King in dentures.

They put one cookbook back on the shelf. Inside its cover they taped a list of the other seven. Mr. Vale had agreed to keep those in his apartment, provided they swore to come get one occasionally so his sister could see a gap.

In the space they’d cleared, the twins stood the other books facing outward. They folded a cereal box into a pocket and tied it firmly to the shelf bracket with a spare shoelace. Borrowing cards went in the pocket. On the front of the shelf, they clipped a second kind of card, where anybody could tell the next reader something worth knowing.

Mrs. Santiago wrote, *Nico ate four pancakes. He says they count as green because we used a green bowl.*

The man from the fifth floor wrote, *Look up the fish with the lightbulb on its head. Imagine trying to sleep next to your wife with that thing on.*

In the empty space where their comic belonged, Milo clipped Ada’s card. Beneath it Inez added a scrap of paper for people who wanted a turn. Mr. Vale signed up first.

“I want to try the voice,” he explained.

When Mrs. Bell came downstairs, Nico was showing Mr. Vale the dent in the pot. Mrs. Santiago was trying to convince the man from the fifth floor to draw an anglerfish on her son’s lunch bag. Milo was holding one end of a fitted sheet for Ada’s grandpa, who had come down to dry some pillowcases and been challenged to prove a claim.

Grandpa tucked one corner inside another, shook the sheet twice, and laid a neat white rectangle on the clear folding table.

Mrs. Bell put a hand to her heart.

“Well?” Inez asked.

Mrs. Bell inspected the shelf. She tugged on the card pocket. She read Ada’s review and added her name below Mr. Vale’s.

“I think you’d better keep the library,” she said. “Looks like I’m third in line for a book.”

That Monday, Inez told the school librarian about their shelf. The librarian still wouldn’t let her work the circulation desk, but she did ask which book Inez would recommend for a boy who claimed he hated reading.

“Does he have a grandpa with removable teeth?” Inez asked.

Milo rode the elevator home that afternoon with three people. One asked him for the pancake recipe. One wanted to know whether Butterscotch survived the blizzard in book two. One simply said, “Holding the elevator?”

“Yep,” said Milo.

Then the doors slid open, and from somewhere down the hall six voices shouted, “INITIATE CHILI SEQUENCE!”

Their favorite comic was back.
