# The Note Underneath

Mara’s clarinet case was on the chair I’d been looking at for six weeks.

I knew where she’d been. Her family had gone to visit her grandmother, and their flight home was supposed to land after the spring concert. I also knew the chair was exactly the same as mine, right down to the wad of gum stuck underneath. Still, all the way to school that morning I’d been picturing myself sitting in it.

“We got in last night,” Mara said. “I slept in my jeans.”

She looked it. There was a crease down one side of her face, and she’d put her reed on crooked. While she fixed it, I set my music on the stand. The second clarinet part landed on top. Its corners had gone soft from six weeks of being carried home inside my maths book. Underneath was the first part Ms. Vale had given me on Monday.

“Were you going to play first?” Mara asked.

“I was going to try it today.”

“Oh,” she said. She took the reed off again and turned it over.

Ms. Vale came in carrying the kitchen timer she used for sectional rehearsals. Behind her were Tessa with her flute and Dev with his cello, which he had to carry sideways to get past the doorstop.

“We’ve got twenty minutes before the choir needs the room,” Ms. Vale said. “By the time we leave, I need to know which version of ‘The Footbridge’ to put on your stands tonight.”

She held up two scores. The blue one was the original, with Mara on first and me on second. In that version the first clarinet carried a long tune over the cello’s plucked notes. My part was mostly on the offbeats, with four measures near the end where I got to come out from under everything.

The green score was the version Ms. Vale had made for three players when she thought Mara would be away. The cello bowed a short, repeating figure, and the first clarinet and flute tossed pieces of the tune back and forth. I’d been given the first clarinet part because I was the only clarinetist we expected to have. Ms. Vale had kept a second clarinet line in small print, marked *optional*. Mara bent over the green page to see what she’d be playing.

“I double Dev here?” she asked.

“An octave up, and only where you have notes,” Ms. Vale said. “See how much you can hear of his bow while you’re playing.”

She set the timer. It began to tick much louder than a thing that small should have been able to tick.

We played the green version first.

I’d practiced my opening four measures in my room until I could play them facing the wardrobe with the music behind me. At school, with Dev bowing underneath, the notes wanted to go a little faster than I’d practiced them. I went with him. By the time Tessa took over, we’d found a speed that made me feel as though I could get on a bike and take both feet off the ground.

Then I came in a beat early.

Tessa lowered her flute. “You cut off my bit.”

“I thought you were done.”

“I have one more note. The tiny one.”

It was a full-sized note. She meant it was short. We started two measures back, and this time I waited to hear it. Tessa flicked it up at the end of her phrase, and my answer came out so naturally I almost laughed into the mouthpiece. At the repeat, Dev dug into the low string and Mara came in above him. I could hear the bow catch before her note sounded. The two sounds together gave the whole thing a shove.

When we finished, Dev kept his bow in the air.

“That one’s fun,” he said.

I looked over at Mara. She was penciling a breath into her part.

We had eleven minutes left.

For the blue version, Dev put his bow across his knees. He plucked the first note, and Mara came in above it. Her first high note wavered as she got there. I knew the tune well enough to hear what she was aiming for. I’d played it on Monday night until my downstairs neighbor knocked on the radiator.

I came in on the offbeat and played too loudly. Mara’s next note disappeared somewhere inside mine.

Ms. Vale tapped my stand. “A shade less, Ivo. Let her finish the shape.”

I dropped so low on the next entrance that my reed gave a faint, horrible squeak. Dev’s mouth twitched. I spent the next eight measures looking hard at the music.

We made it to the place near the end where Mara’s line stopped and mine continued. At home I always took a fresh breath there, even when I meant to play the whole phrase through. There was nobody in my room to leave hanging in the silence.

This time Mara’s last note was still in the air when I reached mine. It was the same note, in a lower octave. I had enough breath. I kept going. For four measures the tune went exactly where it had always gone, and I heard, for the first time, how far down it traveled before Tessa came in. She entered so gently I could feel her note in the sound of mine before I could pick it out.

We finished. Someone from the choir tried the door and let it swing shut again.

“Five minutes,” Ms. Vale said. “Play those last eight bars both ways.”

In green, Tessa had the ending, with little jabs from my clarinet between her notes. We got them exactly together on the second try. I could imagine the front row sitting up at that sound. In blue, I lost the thread as soon as Mara stopped. I’d been waiting so hard for the moment I’d liked the first time that I missed the breath she took before it.

“Again?” I asked.

Mara nodded. This time I watched the edge of her sleeve rise. When she let her note go, I was there.

Ms. Vale turned the timer over. “What are we playing?”

Dev voted green. He liked using the bow. Tessa voted blue. She said she could hear everybody at the end. Mara ran one finger down the optional part, where there was a whole stretch of empty measures.

“Blue,” she said. “I like having somebody to hand it to.”

It bothered me that I knew exactly what she meant. I still wanted to play the opening of the green version in front of people. I wanted to get Tessa’s little note right with an audience there.

“Blue,” I said. “Can we start a bit slower than we did just now?”

Dev plucked the opening twice, leaving more space the second time. I nodded. It was the last sound we made before the timer went off.

We had forty minutes before the concert. In the canteen, I ate half a cheese sandwich and reread the green part. I got as far as the place where I’d come in early. Tessa had drawn a small arrow on my page pointing to the beat where her note would be. I rubbed a crumb off it with my thumb.

Mara sat down across from me and put her hands flat on the table.

“Are your fingers cold?” she asked.

They weren’t. Hers were. She’d washed them in the bathroom, where the hot tap only worked if you held it down with your elbow. I slid my paper cup of tea over to her. She wrapped her hands around it.

“I heard you from the corridor before I came in,” she said. “You were playing the beginning.”

“Did you hear me miss the high note?”

“I thought that was the door.”

I laughed. Then I folded the green part once and put it in my case. I could still have played the opening from memory. I tried the fingerings under the table until Ms. Vale came to get us.

Onstage, the lights made it hard to see past the second row. I’d spent the break imagining exactly where my dad would be sitting. Now all I could find was one of his shoes sticking into the aisle. He always crossed his ankle over his knee at the movies, too.

Dev put his thumb against the string. From my chair I could see the small dent it made in the pad of his thumb before he let go. I watched for the next one. The slower pace gave each plucked note time to get all the way out into the hall before mine came in.

Mara reached the high note cleanly. A little farther on, she took a breath in a place she hadn’t in rehearsal. I heard the gap and almost pushed my next note into it. Then Tessa’s flute came through from the other side of the stand. There had been room for her there all along. I eased back enough to hear where she ended.

Near the last page turn someone in the audience coughed twice. I looked up anyway. Mara’s sleeve rose. On the green page I’d spent the break reading, I would have had two quick notes right there, with Dev’s bow under me. Here he plucked once and lifted his hand. I could hear the faint sound of Mara’s keys as she came down to the note she would leave me.

For those four bars I could see my dad’s shoe, the pale edge of Tessa’s music, and the place where my own thumb had worn the corner of the page smooth. I’d been playing the phrase to my wardrobe for weeks, always speeding up a little at the end because there was nothing after it in my room. This time I let the last note take its full length. Tessa came in while I still had air, and for the last beat I could hear her better than I could hear myself.

We held still. In the dark beyond the second row somebody drew a breath. Then came the clapping, and Dev had to nudge my shoe with his before I remembered to stand.
