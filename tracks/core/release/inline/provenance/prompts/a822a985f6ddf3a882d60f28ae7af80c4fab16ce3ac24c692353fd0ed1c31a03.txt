The first Saturday the bus stopped going down Linden Street, Mina still put six egg rolls in the oven at seven ten. She had made them in batches of six for nine years. At seven twenty-two the bus would pull up, and the people going on to the hospital or the county home would come in while the driver took his break. They bought whatever she could put in a bag with one hand. She used to crack the eggs before she had her coat off.

At seven twenty-two she heard the ice machine drop a load in the fish shop across the street. The rolls were ready. She put them under the warmer and went out with the broom.

The town had taken the bus sign and left the post. In the bakery window, at the height of a seated passenger’s head, there was a greasy oval where people cupped their hands to see whether she was open. She had washed it on Friday and there was already a new one. Mr. Amato, who rode out to see his wife at the county home, stood at the post in his good shoes.

“Recreation center,” Mina said. “Three blocks up.”

“I know. I wanted an egg.”

She went in and bagged one. By the time she brought it out he had started walking, so she carried it after him, her apron strings loose. At the corner he paid her in quarters. For the length of Linden she could see the new shelter, set back in the recreation center lot where the bus could turn without having to back up. Its roof was the same green as the lids on the town trash cans. A woman in scrubs was sitting inside it. From where Mina stood the woman looked like somebody Mina knew, but every woman in scrubs looked like somebody she knew at that distance.

By eight she had sold two rolls. She ate one over the sink and put the last three in the refrigerator. The eggs would make the bread wet by lunchtime.

Next door, Cal turned the library key at eight exactly. The first thing he did on Saturdays was listen for the toilet in the staff washroom. If it was running, he lifted the tank lid and pushed the float down and up again. Then he wheeled the cart of picture books into the children’s room and took the chairs off the tables.

At nine he opened the front door. An hour was enough if he did things in order. The room doubled as a meeting room on Friday evenings, and the people from the model railroad club had long arms and saw no reason to leave aisles between the tables. Cal liked to get down on one knee after moving the furniture. The second Saturday Mina made three egg rolls. She used the other half of the baking sheet for the little buns she sold by the dozen after ten. At seven twenty-two she stayed inside and scraped the dried dough off her worktable.

A woman came in at seven thirty with a canvas lunch bag and asked if the cinnamon twists had gone up. Mina said they had, by a quarter, and the woman took one anyway. It was the woman from the shelter. Mina knew her now by the yellow laces in her work shoes.

“I could’ve had one last week,” the woman said. “I couldn’t tell if you’d be open this early. Everything down here looked shut.”

Mina had turned on the lights over the counter as she always did, leaving the window lights off until she had set out the bread. The old bus had stopped so close that passengers could see right in to the counter.

“You got time to eat it here?” Mina asked.

“Seven minutes.”

There was one chair by the bakery window, where Mina sat to enter invoices into her phone. It had a good view of the road and no view of the shelter. The woman took the twist with her. When Mina went to retrieve the chalkboard she generally put out with the nine o’clock bread, she found a piece of yellow chalk wedged in the hinge. She rubbed away yesterday’s prices with the heel of her hand and wrote **HOT BREAKFAST**. Underneath, smaller, she put **SATURDAYS FROM 7**.

She carried the board to the corner. From there she could see a bus in the recreation center lot and its bright route number. She propped the board against the brick with the writing turned uphill, then went back to the bakery and stood where a customer would stand. She could see the strip of bare sidewalk where the board usually went. She had always assumed that was where she needed it.

Cal came down from the direction of the recreation center at five to eight. He had stopped at the shelter to fish a library book out from under a bench. It was damp on the back but the pages separated. As he came around the corner with it open on his palm, he nearly stepped on the board.

Mina saw him and came out. He held up the book. It was the one about the mitten. The front had been dry enough for him to recognize it from several feet away. He had left it on display all week, and someone had checked it out on Thursday.

“You can dry that on my rack,” Mina said.

In the bakery she spread a paper bag on the cooling rack and set the book on it with the covers open. Cal stood near the chair, holding his keys.

“I sent a man over last week,” she said. “His little girl was cold.”

“I saw them.”

“I told him you were there because I’d heard you come in.”

Cal pulled the chair out a little with his foot. “It takes me till nine to get the room ready. They can stay all morning after that.”

Mina squared up the stack of bags beside the register. Through the window she could see the empty bus post and, just behind it, the lip of the curb where bus passengers used to set their grocery bags while they counted their change.

“I could put your hours on my board,” she said. “If you don’t mind them being under the eggs.”

Cal looked at the bakery clock. It was eight now. He could get the room ready in fifty-five minutes if the toilet was quiet.

“Put ‘reading room’ too,” he said. “People know we have books.”

She handed him the yellow chalk. He went out to the corner and crouched. There was room for **LIBRARY OPENS AT 9** if he wrote it on two lines. He used the very bottom of the board for **CHILDREN’S READING ROOM** and had to write the last word twice to fit it. When he stood up, the writing looked low enough for Nora to read, if she could read. He didn’t know how old she was.

Inside the library the toilet was running. He fixed it, wheeled the cart into the children’s room, and set the chairs down one at a time so their legs didn’t strike the floor. He chose a book about a city pigeon for the spot where the mitten book had been. With four minutes left he sat in the chair farthest from the door and read the first two pages to himself. He sometimes did this to see how much of a picture a child could make out straight across the room. The pigeon’s feet were excellent even from there.

At nine Mina heard the bolt slide next door. She had an egg roll left and a man at the counter counting change for coffee. While he counted, a bus came into view at the head of Linden and turned into the lot. A few minutes later a woman in a red hat walked down from the corner, carrying a library book against her hip. She slowed at the bakery window and leaned in with both hands to the glass. Mina lifted the last egg roll with the tongs so she could see it. The woman looked down the street, then came in.
