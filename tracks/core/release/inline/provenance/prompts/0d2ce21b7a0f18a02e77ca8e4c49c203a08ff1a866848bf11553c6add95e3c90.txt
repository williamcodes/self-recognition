# The Saturday Measure

At seven on Saturday, Nessa found three bolts of cloth on the table she had been unfolding at Bell Street Market for twenty years. Her niece had put the heaviest one over the hinge, which was the right place for it. That irritated Nessa more than if she’d put it somewhere foolish.

“Where do you want the coats?” Nessa asked.

Mara looked at the coats on her aunt’s arm. “I thought we could hang those.”

“From what?”

Mara had brought a broom handle and two clamps. She showed Nessa how the handle could go across the back of the table. It held four coats before it began to bow. Nessa had brought nine.

“I’ve got the drill cotton in three colors,” Mara said. “And the ticking came in. I’m selling it by the meter.”

Nessa set the other five coats on the chair. “When did you decide that?”

“Tuesday. I put up a sign.”

The sign was on the bread stall, tucked between the prices of the seeded loaf and the rolls. At noon Mara would demonstrate how to measure and cut a skirt from a length of cloth. There would be a paper pattern to take home. Nessa read the sign twice. On Tuesday Mara had come to supper and taken home half a pear cake in a tin.

Across the aisle, Dev was setting out his eggs. He watched them unfold the fitting screen and came round to move one of its feet with his shoe. The screen had three hinged panels, each covered in yellow curtain offcuts. Where the aisle pinched between their table and his, a person with a shopping trolley had to turn sideways.

“Keep the foot on your side of the drain,” he said. “The woman with the twins nearly took out my mediums last week.”

“We need another screen at noon,” said Mara. “For people to try the sample.”

Dev looked at the first screen and then at Mara, giving her time to look too.

“All right,” she said. “We share it.”

By eight, the coats on the chair had acquired the air of things waiting to be taken home. Nessa fetched two wooden hangers from her van and hooked them over the screen. When the first customer went behind it to try a raincoat, the sleeves reached in after her.

Mrs. Vale came at nine with her husband’s trousers rolled under her arm. Nessa had sold them to him the previous week and pinned the hems while he stood in his socks. She had asked him to come back in the shoes he meant to wear.

“He’s sitting by the bread,” Mrs. Vale said. “It took him twenty minutes to get those shoes on. If you want him, go and fetch him yourself.”

Nessa went. Mr. Vale came slowly and with good humor, eating a roll in small tidy bites. Behind the screen, he handed the rest of it out to his wife. Nessa knelt to turn up one trouser leg, her knuckles brushing the thick leather of his new shoe. When she reached for her chalk, she found that Mara had moved the sewing tin to make space for the ticking.

“Can I leave these on?” Mr. Vale asked. “We’re going for lunch. First time since the operation.”

Nessa said she would have them ready before noon. Mrs. Vale put the last of the roll in her own mouth and began to fold the paper bag into a square.

At ten Mara sold two meters of green cotton to a woman who had come for eggs. The woman wanted to replace the seat of a swing in her garden. She and Mara discussed the weight of a wet child. Mara pulled the cloth taut between her hands and told her where she would double it. Nessa had heard her talk this way when she still worked in the curtain shop. People used to bring measurements written on the backs of envelopes, and Mara could generally find the window in the arithmetic.

After the woman left, Mara wrote the sale in a school exercise book. Beside the date were two figures in red.

“What’s that one?” Nessa asked.

“Wholesale. The other’s what I owe you for the pitch.”

“I thought you were paying me for Saturdays so you could sell what you made.”

“I made six aprons last month. I sold one to you.”

“I use it.”

“I know you use it.” Mara ran a thumbnail along the edge of the book. “The shop paid me for my hours, even if no one bought curtains that day. I’ve got to find something that pays me for more of mine.”

Nessa took Mr. Vale’s trousers out to the van to sew. The passenger seat was high enough to rest her feet on the step, and the light came through the windscreen. She had worked out there in January once, when a pipe burst in the hall where the market spent its winters. She remembered having laughed about it then. Through the glass she could see Mara take down two coats to show a man the blue ticking. Mara folded each coat along its seams before she put it on the chair.

At a quarter to noon, six people were waiting, including Mrs. Vale, who had seen the sign while buying rolls and wanted a skirt she could cycle in. Len from the knife stall brought over a stool for Mr. Vale. Then he stayed beside it with his arms folded. He had sharpened Mara’s shears that morning and wanted to see how they cut.

Nessa gave Mr. Vale the trousers. He took them behind the screen. When he came out, one hem was caught on the back of his shoe. Nessa stooped to free it and heard Mara ask someone to hold the end of the cloth.

The green cotton ran the full length of the table. Mara laid her paper pattern on it and weighted the corners with eggs Dev had brought over in their carton. Then a young woman at the front asked how much fabric she’d need if she wanted the skirt to hang like the sample. Mara looked down at the pattern. She had traced it in her own size.

“How much room do you like when you sit?” Nessa asked the woman.

The woman looked at the people around the table. “Depends where I’m sitting.”

Mr. Vale offered her his stool. She sat and spread her knees a little, as though she had a child standing between them. Mara crouched beside her with the tape and showed her how to check the cloth across her lap. Then she took the pattern off the green cotton, turned it over, and drew a wider version on the back. The others leaned in. Len steadied the carton with one finger as the paper slid under it.

When the young woman came out from behind the screen in the sample skirt, its hem dipped considerably in front. She turned to see what it did at the back. Nessa held up the little mirror she kept for people trying on coats.

“I’d cut yours longer here,” Mara said, touching the side of her own hip. “Then we’d level it while you’re wearing it.”

The young woman bought the blue ticking. She asked if she could come back in a week with the skirt basted and have Mara look at the hem. Mara’s eyes went to Nessa. Nessa was putting the mirror down.

“Come at one,” Mara said.

After the crowd thinned, Dev took his eggs back. One had a crack in it. He held it up to the light and said he’d have it for his tea. Len asked if Mara would make him a new canvas roll for his knives. She told him what she would charge for the sewing, and he said he’d think about it. He came back twenty minutes later to ask how much canvas he should buy.

By three, Nessa had sold two coats. She’d taken in the waist of a pair of work trousers and let out a school blazer she remembered taking in for the same boy in September. Mara had sold from all three bolts. They counted their money at opposite ends of the table while the bread stall packed away. Nessa found that she’d put Mr. Vale’s alteration money in the pocket of the apron Mara had made. She left it there.

“You can have the whole table from twelve next Saturday,” Nessa said. “I want it till then. Screen as well.”

Mara closed her exercise book. “Can I sell a cut length in the morning if somebody asks?”

“If we can clear a space.”

“And you’ll go home at twelve?”

Nessa had been about to say she would stay, that she could bring a chair and do her hand sewing, that there were people who only ever came in the afternoon. She looked at the chair with the coats on it. One had slipped far enough for its cuff to sweep the ground.

“I’ll go and have lunch,” she said. “I can tell people in the morning where I’ll be.”

They turned the bread stall sign over and wrote the hours on the back. Nessa put *Fittings and mended clothes, eight to twelve*. Mara wrote *Fabric by the meter, twelve to four*. There was enough space at the bottom for another line. Mara offered her the pencil.

Nessa thought of the skirt in blue ticking and the woman who would come back wearing it full of pins. “Put your name on it,” she said. “So she knows who to ask for.”

Mara wrote both their names. While Nessa fetched the van, she stood by the folded screen and waited for the pencil marks to stop smudging under her thumb.
