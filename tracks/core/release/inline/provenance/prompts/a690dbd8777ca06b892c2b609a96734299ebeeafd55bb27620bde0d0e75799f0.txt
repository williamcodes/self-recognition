# Reading the timetable together

## Pilot proposal

The museum should offer a one-term weekday pilot built around a question pupils aged nine to eleven can answer for themselves by the end of a visit. “If we have to be at the market by 10 o’clock, which service should we catch?” A paper timetable gives pupils a reason to look closely at a transport object, a problem they can solve together, and a way to check their own reasoning. The pilot would also introduce a simple booking reminder and an easy way for teachers to release or rearrange a date. These changes address two different opportunities in the museum’s figures.

## The 75-minute visit

Each class of up to 30 begins and ends together in Room A. For the middle of the visit, two groups of up to 15 exchange rooms. The educator stays with the timetable activity in Room A. The volunteer runs a rehearsed activity in Room B with a script and answer cards. The teacher can support pupils who need a familiar adult. The sequence requires no third museum worker.

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–8 | **Welcome and first attempt, Room A.** Give each pupil a folded response card. On the front, pupils use a small timetable to find the arrival time of one named service. Collect first attempts before discussing answers. Introduce the market journey and ask what a passenger would need to know before setting out. |
| 8–15 | **Read one journey together.** Display an enlarged timetable with stops down the side and one service in each column. The educator points to the departure stop, chooses a service and traces that column to the destination. A pupil repeats the trace while the class checks that both times belong to the same service. |
| 15–33 | **First rotation.** Half the class works with the educator in Room A and half with the volunteer in Room B. |
| 33–36 | **Change rooms.** Pupils leave the materials set out for the next group. The adults direct the exchange and give each group its new journey brief. |
| 36–54 | **Second rotation.** Each group completes the other activity. |
| 54–65 | **Make the market connection, Room A.** Reunite the class. In pairs, pupils choose the latest service that reaches the market by 10 o’clock and prepare to show their evidence. Invite two pairs to trace their choices. Ask whether an earlier service would also work and what the passenger would gain or lose by taking it. |
| 65–75 | **Independent exit and departure.** Give pupils a fresh timetable problem of the same difficulty as the first attempt, followed by the museum’s existing exit question. Pupils may point or explain orally if that is their usual way of responding. Reserve the final two minutes to collect cards and thank the class. |

In **Room A**, pairs act as passenger and booking clerk. The passenger draws a request such as “I’m leaving School Lane after 09:10 and need to reach Market Hall by 10:00.” The clerk places two counters on the timetable and reads back the stop and time under each. The passenger checks the journey before accepting a reusable ticket. Pupils swap roles for a second request. The educator watches for a revealing mistake, choosing a departure in one column and an arrival in the next, and asks the pair to trace the service between their counters.

In **Room B**, the volunteer gives each small group a reproduction of a collection timetable or a photograph of one, alongside a clearly labelled teaching copy. Pupils look for clues a passenger would have used, including the direction of travel, the stop names and the days of operation. They then test a fictional passenger’s claim against the teaching copy. One passenger, for example, has promised to arrive at the time printed for the stop *before* Market Hall. Pupils identify the mistake and correct the message. The volunteer’s answer card shows the route a finger should trace, so a pupil who gives an unexpected answer can demonstrate how they reached it.
