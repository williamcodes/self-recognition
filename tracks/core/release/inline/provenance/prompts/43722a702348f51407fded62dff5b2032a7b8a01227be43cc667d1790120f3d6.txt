# The Second Bell

On Saturdays Mina unlocked the bakery by touch. There were three keys on the ring, and the one for the street door had a burr that caught the pad of her thumb. She liked to get all the way to the kitchen before she turned on a light. For those few steps the ovens might still be new, the floor level, the envelope from the flour company something other than a bill.

On the first Saturday in April she had forty-eight egg buns in the oven before she remembered about the bus.

She stood with her hand on the timer. She had made forty-eight by dividing the dough in half until she had sixteen pieces, then doing it twice more. Her hands knew Saturday as well as they knew the keys.

Outside, the town had left the pole and taken the bench. A white sleeve fitted over the bus sign directed riders to the recreation center, three blocks east. The new stop had a shelter, a curb a driver could lower a ramp onto, and room for two buses to pass. Mina had been at the meeting. She had seen the photograph of a woman in a wheelchair waiting in the mouth of the alley because there was nowhere else on Linden Street for her to go.

Still, when the timer rang, there were forty-eight.

Next door, at eight, Cal opened the library’s street door, came inside, and locked it behind him. He had an hour until opening. He pulled the returns bin away from the slot, raised the shades, and went to the children’s room with the little red level he kept in his desk. The building had once housed a men’s clothing store, and any furniture small enough for a child had all four feet on a different part of the old floor.

For six Saturdays a bus had discharged its passengers just as Cal raised the shades. Somebody always saw him. A father would hold up a little girl, the little girl would wave, and sooner or later Cal would open the door. The last time, he had been on his knees folding a shim out of the library newsletter while two boys tried to ride the low book trolley down the slope toward biographies.

That first quiet morning he discovered the wobble in the green table came from a loose screw. He tightened it with a dime. He washed a winter’s worth of gray fingerprints from the window seat. At nine precisely he turned the lock and put the doorstop under the door. A little girl named Pearl came in with her grandfather and laid both hands on the green table as if to take its pulse.

“My grandpa’s getting his ears done,” she said.

“Hearing aids,” her grandfather said. “She makes it sound like I’m a spaniel.”

At the bakery Mina had begun to notice things she usually missed. The jewelry-store man crossed the street in his slippers to get his paper. A crow dropped something hard onto the manhole cover, waited for a car to pass, then went down to see what had opened. At ten minutes past nine, a woman came around the corner pulling a plaid shopping cart. Mina had been selling her a bun and a small coffee for years without learning her name.

“Thought we lost you,” Mina said.

“Almost lost my bus. I stood out there reading that sign like it might change its mind.”

She was on her way home from an overnight shift at the county laundry. The next bus was at nine thirty-five. She had time to sit for once. She took the chair by the radiator, set the bun on its paper, and fell asleep with a hand laid over it.

Mina woke her at nine twenty-five. The woman went out chewing, one arm still looking for the sleeve of her coat.

By noon Mina had sold nineteen buns. She took six next door for the library staff and remembered, when Cal met her at the desk, that on Saturdays the staff was Cal.

“Take them home,” she said.

“I live alone.”

“You think I live with twenty-nine people?”

They ate one apiece at the desk. Through the open door of the children’s room she could see Pearl stretched on her stomach, her shoes kicked off, working her finger along a line of print. Her grandfather was asleep in the window seat.

“I got the whole room done this morning,” Cal said. He sounded so pleased that Mina spared him the remark she had ready about the town doing somebody a favor.

She gave four buns to the tattoo shop on the corner. At closing she split the rest and put them in the freezer. They would make a breakfast pudding, though she had never known anybody to come in off the street with a hankering for breakfast pudding.

The second Saturday she baked twenty-four. She also made a small pan of the pudding, with sharp cheddar and the last two leeks from the refrigerator. Cutting the dark green tops off the leeks, she heard the soft squeal of little wheels outside and looked up in time to see the plaid cart go by, headed east.

It was seven forty. The woman had nearly an hour until the eight thirty-five. Mina stepped into the doorway with the knife still in her hand.

“You’re early.”

“The shelter seats six,” the woman called back. “You want a seat, you go.”

It had rained overnight. A wet thread ran down the middle of the empty windowsill where people used to set their coffee while they dug out their passes.

At eight Cal came up Linden carrying his lunch in a pickle jar, which meant soup. Mina could tell quite a lot about his week from the container he carried on Saturday. On a good week he brought something in a square glass dish with a blue snap lid. Once, in February, he’d come swinging a sleeve of crackers.

“People are sitting down there for an hour,” she told him.

He stopped with his key in the library door. “In this weather?”

“I’m thinking of taking a tray down.”

“I’d buy one before you go.”

“Taking the tray’s the easy part. Who’s here selling the rest?”

Cal looked at the buns, then at the library shades, still down. Mina could see him getting ready to give away his hour. She had watched him give it away in pieces all winter.

“Go fix something,” she said.

Inside, Cal put his soup in the refrigerator and began his rounds. He had planned to move the books about families out of the old revolving rack. You couldn’t turn the thing without pinching a finger, and children had a natural conviction that anything on a spindle ought to go very fast. He emptied the rack, wiped the books and stood them in baskets under the window seat.

At eight forty-five he was finished. He sat in the little room a minute and heard, through the common wall, the bakery bell. One note when the door opened, another when it shut. He heard it twice, close together. Then nothing for a long while.

At nine he propped open the front door. Pearl’s grandfather appeared alone. Pearl was coming on the ten o’clock bus with her mother, he said. He’d come ahead to get the window seat.

“You like that spot,” Cal said.

“I can hear her read there. Glass behind me throws her voice back a little.”

He had brought a cushion from home.

At a quarter past nine Mina brought Cal a square of pudding in a paper boat. She had sold two to the jewelry-store man, who wanted something he could eat with a fork while his front tooth was away being matched. She had also sold three buns. Cal ate standing up, looking across the desk at the empty revolving rack.

“Do you still have the picnic basket?” he asked.

It was a wide basket with two lids, left from the summer Mina had tried selling complicated lunches. Under her counter it held string, spare aprons and a box of birthday candles shaped like numerals. She fetched it. Cal took the Saturday timetable from beside the copier. Between nine fifteen and nine thirty-five two buses came into the recreation center, one from the county hospital and one from the apartments out by the reservoir.

“I could go now,” Mina said.

The problem of the bakery remained. Cal turned the pudding boat around, scraping the corners with his fork. From where he stood he could see Mina’s till through two thicknesses of plate glass. He could see the stool behind it and the blackboard on the wall. The prices were too small to read.

“Lock up for twenty minutes,” he said. “Put your chalkboard outside. If anybody wants to wait, they can come in here.”

Mina considered the three buns she had sold in two hours. Then she took the fork from him. “You’re eating the paper.”

She put twelve buns in the basket and chalked *Back at 9:40. You’re welcome to wait at the library next door* on the board. At the last minute she stuck the card reader in her apron. Cal went back to his desk. Within a minute the jewelry-store man came in to ask whether Mina would be bringing back any more of the leek stuff. Cal gave him a pencil and a slip of paper to write down what he wanted. Then he gave him another slip because the first had become a sketch of his missing tooth.

At the shelter Mina sold seven buns. The woman with the plaid cart had gone, but a man in laundry blues bought two, one to eat on the bus and one for when he woke up. A boy counted out the price in wet quarters from the pocket of his swim trunks. Mina had forgotten napkins. She made a mental note. So little of running a bakery was baking.

When she came back, the library door stood open. From the window seat she could hear Pearl’s grandfather reading aloud, practicing the different voices. Cal handed her the order for pudding.

“Same time next week?” he asked.

“After you open,” she said.

She took her board back in and rang up the seven sales. Next Saturday she would take the napkins and leave the coffee. Twelve buns fit the basket without crushing the tops. She could decide about the other twelve when she got home tonight and sat down, when her hands had finished telling her what to do.

Through the wall came a little thump, then a child’s voice saying, with deep satisfaction, “See? It doesn’t even wiggle.”
