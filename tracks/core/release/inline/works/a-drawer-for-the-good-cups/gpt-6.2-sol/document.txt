# Six Plain Cups

At nine on Sunday, Nora found her brother standing on the kitchen stool, eating cherries out of a bowl he had already priced.

“Those are Dad’s,” she said.

“He told me to finish them.”

“The bowl.”

Paul looked down at the strip of masking tape on its rim. He had written $3 in the square black figures he used on birthday envelopes. “He told me to sell that.”

On the counter their father had left a note in the same hand that had excused Nora from swimming thirty years ago. *Kitchen radio. Six plain cups. Key to Mrs. Alvarez.* In the smaller apartment he had two burners, a refrigerator under the counter, and a window he could open with one hand. This last feature had figured prominently in his account of the move. The windows here had always required a knife.

“Leave the stool out of the sale,” Paul said. “I could use it.”

Nora had already measured the stool against the space between her refrigerator and the wall. It was thirteen inches wide with its legs folded. She had measured it the previous Sunday while her father slept in his chair, then put the tape measure back in its drawer.

“Let’s leave it out for now,” she said.

Paul gave her the bowl and reached into the cupboard for the cups. Standing up there, with his socks slipping back from his heels, he told her he used to make her toast before school. She remembered their father making it. He cut it into strips that fitted through the handle of a cup.

“He made it when he was home,” Paul said. “When he was on earlies, I did.”

“Did you cut it up?”

“Of course I cut it up. You couldn’t be given a whole piece of anything.”

He handed down two white cups, then a third with a fur of dust on the handle. Nora tried to picture Paul at fourteen with a bread knife in his hand. She could get the knife right, the brown handle with one rivet missing, but she kept giving him their father’s wrist.

By ten, their sister had arrived with packing paper and a roast chicken. Elise put the chicken in the refrigerator, which they had unplugged at nine. Nora plugged it back in.

In the living room Elise pulled the sofa forward and found a blue glass bead. She held it up to the window. Outside, someone was hanging a sheet over the railing of the building opposite. Its damp edge darkened the concrete.

“I used to sit back here when Mum called,” Elise said.

Their mother had moved out when Nora was eight. For years the telephone stayed on a little shelf in the living room, and everything said on it could be heard from the kitchen.

“You used to take the phone into the hall,” Nora said.

“After we got the long cord.” Elise rolled the bead between her fingers. “Before that I sat behind the sofa. I thought if Dad couldn’t see me he couldn’t hear me.”

Nora pulled at a loose thread in the sofa arm. She remembered lying on those cushions while Elise talked, her face turned toward the back so Elise would think she was asleep. Their mother had a way of asking what they’d had for supper that made Nora wish there were something better to report. Sometimes she said chicken when they’d had toast. Once she said they’d eaten a whole fish and then couldn’t answer when her mother asked what kind.

“Did she ever ask to talk to me when I was asleep?” she said.

“Most times. I used to wake you. You were horrible about it.”

Nora wound the thread around her finger and broke it. Elise put the bead in the pocket of her jeans. Then she went to the kitchen for a knife to cut the tape on a carton and came back carrying the stool.

“I’d like this,” she said. “For the front hall. The children sit right in the doorway to put their shoes on.”

“Paul wants it.”

“Paul can have the sofa.”

At noon they ate the chicken over the sink, passing the salt from hand to hand. The buyer had agreed to take the furniture, the boxed kitchenware, and whatever books were left at four. Everything else had to fit in Elise’s hatchback or go down to the building’s rubbish room. Their father had arranged for the city to collect his mattress on Monday. He had tied a note to it with string, although no one who saw the mattress leaning in the corridor would have been in any doubt about what it was for.

In the small bedroom, Paul opened the wardrobe and found a row of pencil marks on the inside of the door. His name stopped at five foot nine. Elise’s last mark was dated the June she graduated. Nora’s went on until she was twenty, the final one in her own handwriting.

“You kept doing it?” Paul said.

“I wanted to see if I’d get taller.”

“After twenty?”

“I started late.”

He shut the door, then opened it again and took a photograph. Nora slid the empty hangers along the rod. After Elise left, she had moved into this room and dragged the stool in from the kitchen to use as a bedside table. It had held a lamp, then a glass of water, then a bowl for the copper coins she shook out of her work trousers. In those years her father had got into the habit of knocking on her door at ten. If she was awake he asked whether she wanted tea. If she was asleep, he left her alone. She knew this because sometimes she let the knock go unanswered and listened to him walk away.

“You could take the door,” Paul said.

“Where would I put a door?”

He considered it. “That is the trouble with a door.”

They took the hangers down to the rubbish room. When they came back Elise was sitting on the stool in the bare patch of carpet where the sofa had stood. She had wrapped five cups and was looking at a sixth. It had a thin green line around the rim.

“That counts as plain,” Paul said.

Nora called their father. He picked up after one ring and told her which cupboard they were in.

“We’ve got five of those,” she said. “We can give you one with a green rim.”

“Is there one in the bathroom?”

There was. It held his shaving brush. A little soap had dried in the bottom. Nora rinsed it while he told her that the fellow across the landing had lent him a drill. The radio would go on a shelf by the window, where he’d tried his phone and got all the stations he wanted.

“Are you putting the shelf up today?” she asked.

“He’s putting it up. I’m holding the screws.”

Back in the kitchen she dried the cup, packed it, and got the radio down. The cord had gone stiff in the shape it had held for years, with a kink where it bent around the bread bin.

At four the buyer knocked with two men behind him. He opened the cartons and ran his thumb along the rims of the plates. He was wearing a good pale shirt, and after a while the dust showed on his stomach where he leaned against things. He named a price. Paul looked at Elise, who looked at the clock on her phone, and said yes.

“What about that?” the buyer asked, pointing to the stool.

“That’s mine,” Nora said.

The buyer turned to ask one of the men about the wardrobe. Nora picked up the stool and carried it into the kitchen. Elise came in after her.

“Since when?”

Nora set it down. One of its feet landed in the worn place in the linoleum, and it steadied. “I’ve wanted it since Dad told us he was moving.”

“You could have said.”

“I thought you’d both say you wanted it.”

Elise gave a short laugh. “Well, I did.”

From the hall came the slow bump of the sofa against the door frame. Paul called out to ask where they’d put the screwdriver. Elise reached behind the radio carton and handed it to him as he came in.

“Nora’s taking the stool,” she said.

Paul looked at Nora. “Will you actually stand on it?”

“Probably. I might put a lamp on it.”

He turned the screwdriver over in his hand. “There’s a screw coming out underneath. Tip it up.”

She held the stool while he tightened the screw, then the other three. He tested the legs by pushing them apart. Elise went out to hold the front door, and Paul handed the stool back to Nora.

By the time the buyer’s truck pulled away, they could hear Mrs. Alvarez running water through the kitchen wall. Nora carried the key next door. Mrs. Alvarez was doing her washing-up and dried both hands before accepting it. She asked whether their father had got the radio. For years she had heard the six o’clock news through the wall while she made supper, she said. Sometimes she turned hers down.

Downstairs, Paul and Elise were trying to close the hatch without crushing the carton of cups. Nora laid the stool flat and slid it beneath the passenger seat. There was room then to turn the carton so the lid would close.

They took the radio and the cups to their father first. The shelf was up, slightly higher than Nora would have put it. Her father plugged in the radio and turned the dial while she unwrapped the cups. A man was reading out football scores. Paul opened the cupboard over the sink and began putting the cups away, two at a time.

“Leave three down,” their father said. “The kettle’s on.”

There were two chairs at the table. Nora went back to the car for the stool. By the time she got upstairs, Paul had taken his tea to the window and Elise had pulled out a chair for her. Nora unfolded the stool beside the table and sat. From there she could see the screws under the new shelf, their heads bright against the wood. She put both feet on the floor and took the cup her father held out to her.
