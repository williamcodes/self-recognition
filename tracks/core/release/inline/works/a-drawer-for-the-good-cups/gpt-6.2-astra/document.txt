At nine on Sunday morning, Nell put six plain cups on the counter and discovered that one of them said WORLD’S BEST GRANDPA on the other side. None of them had children.

“He bought it for the handle,” Ruth said. “Put your fingers through. You can get all four in.”

Nell tried it. You could. She put it back in the cupboard and found a sixth plain cup behind the salt. Their father had moved on Thursday with his bed, his clothes, a table and one chair. *Kitchen radio and six plain cups,* he had written on the back of his change-of-address form. The buyer from the estate-sale company was coming at four. Anything they wanted for themselves had to fit in Martin’s hatchback, which already contained a spare tire too large for the well intended for it.

Ruth wrapped the cups. Martin unplugged the radio, which fell silent in the middle of the weather. For the first time Nell could hear the little sucking noise the refrigerator made when somebody shut its door.

“I’ll take the stool,” she said.

“I came for the stool,” Martin said.

Ruth stopped winding newspaper around a cup. “Well, so did I.”

It was the low kitchen stool, a square of beech on four splayed legs. Somebody had once painted the legs yellow without turning it over, so that each foot ended in a ragged brown sock. It had no particular value, which was why none of them had thought to stake a claim ahead of time. Ruth set it in the hall.

“We’ll come back to it,” she said, in the voice with which she used to remove a wasp from a room.

They began in the front bedroom. Their father had left the hangers, each with a dry cleaner’s paper bib. Ruth slipped off the bibs and folded them together. On the carpet where the bed had been lay a single brass collar stay. Nell picked it up. It looked like something a bird might have inside its wing.

“Do you remember how he’d stand here on Friday nights,” she said, “and let us choose his tie?”

“Friday mornings,” Ruth said. “He worked the late shift Fridays.”

“He took Mum dancing.”

“He did sometimes. You’re thinking of the photograph.”

Nell could see the whole thing, their mother fastening a little gold bag that closed like a kiss. Then she saw the white margin around it.

Ruth sat down on the carpet. With the bed gone the room seemed low and wide, a place intended for some altogether different animal. “I used to do my homework in here,” she said. “On that stool. I’d pull his drawer out for a desk.”

“Why didn’t you use the kitchen table?”

“Because then somebody would see me.”

“Doing homework?”

“Available,” Ruth said.

She had been seventeen when Nell was six. Nell remembered hunting her through the apartment with a book whose paper flaps had all come off, wanting Ruth to do the voice of the bear. It had never occurred to her that some of the times she failed to find her sister, her sister had been at home.

“What did you do when we came in?”

“Held the drawer up. The runner squeaked if I let go.”

Ruth put the collar stay into one of the paper bibs and folded it shut. Nell let her. They had already thrown away eleven collar stays.

In the bathroom Martin knelt to undo the chrome shelf their father had installed above the sink. Their building superintendent wanted the original tile left bare. Nell stood in the bath with her shoes off, catching the screws in her palm. Under each bracket the green tile was a shade darker, as if wet.

“This was the best room,” Martin said.

Nell looked at the toilet with its lid tied to the seat by a loop of dental floss. “Was it.”

“Listen.”

He struck the towel rail with the screwdriver handle and hummed the note it gave back. For a second the pitch wavered. Then the whole small room seemed to be humming, right into the bones around Nell’s eyes.

“You could get a proper low G in here,” he said. “At school I had to sing alto until Christmas. Here I was practically a man.”

She remembered him occupying the bathroom for hours, their father beating the door with the flat of his hand. The story told at meals was that Martin had been in love with his own reflection. Even Martin told it that way.

“Dad used to sit on the stool outside,” he said. “After Mum died. Did you know? He’d get home from work and I’d be in here and he’d say, ‘Do the one with the river.’ He had to put his ear right down by the keyhole. I wouldn’t sing with the door open.”

“What was the one with the river?”

“I knew you’d ask me.”

He sang two lines. It was an advertisement for a building society. They laughed so hard that Nell opened her hand and lost a screw down the plughole.

At half past one they ate bread and cheese standing up in the kitchen. Nell had spent more time alone with their father than the other two put together, or so she had always reckoned it. After they left home there had been nine years when she had him to herself. She got the stool down from the hall and sat on it to eat. At once her knees came up too high and the counter cut off her view of the window. She had forgotten what a low opinion of the world the stool obliged you to take.

She carried it into the little back room where she had slept. The others followed with their bread. There was just room for the three of them now the narrow bed had gone.

“Here,” she said, placing the stool against the wall. “He used to sit on it while I fell asleep.”

She remembered his cuffs undone, his bare forearms. She could still put him there as easily as putting a glass on a table.

“He’d wait till I was all the way off. Sometimes I’d wake up an hour later and there he’d be.”

“You were a lucky little sod,” Martin said, quite tenderly.

Then Ruth sat on the stool. She leaned sideways until her temple rested against the wall, and closed her eyes. Nell knew that posture. More particularly she knew the hand hanging down, the middle two fingers slightly curled. She had held those fingers. Her father was missing the tip of the middle one, a factory accident from before any of them were born.

“Oh,” Nell said. “Was it you?”

“It was cold against my head. I remember that.”

“Every night?”

“How would I know now?”

Ruth got up to take her plate to the kitchen. A few crumbs clung to the seat. Nell wanted to call her back and make her sit again. There had been nights when it was their father. He had worn a watch with luminous hands, and in the dark she had seen the time go by on his wrist. She had that much still, though she was beginning to wonder what it had cost him to stay awake and whether in fact he had.

By three the hatchback held the radio, the cups, Martin’s records and a rolled rug that pressed its blunt nose to the windscreen until they moved it. Ruth had taken almost nothing. Nell had taken a blue jug she liked perfectly well. Upstairs, the stool stood in the middle of the bare back room.

“Ruth should have it,” Nell said to Martin.

“Did you ask her?”

“She’s the eldest. She did all that sitting.”

“I can hear you,” Ruth called. She appeared in the doorway carrying her coat. “I’m fairly well sick of getting things because I did the work. I got Mum’s sewing machine on precisely those grounds. It takes two men to lift it.”

“Do you actually want the stool?” Nell asked.

“Yes. I like the yellow feet.”

Somehow that settled it for Nell, though in an unexpected direction. “I want it at my house,” she said. “I want to put my foot on it when I do up my shoe. I don’t have a story about that yet.”

Martin considered this. “Could I have it after you?”

“When I die?”

“I was thinking Easter.”

They worked it out on the wall calendar, still hanging in the kitchen on a picture hook. Nell would take it until Easter, when Martin usually came to hers for lunch. Martin would take it to Ruth’s in August for her birthday. Ruth could keep it until she felt like bringing it back. Ruth tore off the current page, folded it and put it in her pocket.

“I reserve the right to paint the top,” she said.

At four the buyer arrived wearing good felt slippers over her shoes and went through the rooms touching the furniture gently, as if waking people on a train. The three of them accepted her offer. Downstairs, the stool fitted over the spare tire with two legs through the handles of the bag of cups.

They drove to their father’s new place first. He had set his table by the window, and the one chair faced a plane tree full of sparrows. Martin plugged in the radio. Ruth put the cups on a shelf within easy reach. Their father counted them.

“Next time you come,” he said, “there’s a big one I like. Says something daft on it.”

“The woman’s got it now, Dad,” Ruth said.

“Oh, well.” He put three fingers through the handle of a plain cup and let his little finger stick out grandly. “How’s this?”

Nell made tea. There was nowhere for everybody to sit, so she went down to the car and fetched the stool. She put it beside her father’s chair. From that height she could see under the branches of the plane tree to the pavement opposite, where a girl was trying to walk along a low wall with her eyes shut. Every few steps she put out a hand, and the woman beside her lifted a finger for her to find.

When they left, Nell remembered her coat and forgot the stool. She got as far as the street before she missed it. Martin was starting the car. She held up one finger and went back for it.
