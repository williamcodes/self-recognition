The first thing Ada and Kit grew together was a quarrel over six inches.

On the first Wednesday in May, Ada drove a stake into the ground and Kit said, “That’s the path.”

“It’s the edge of the path.”

“My foot’s on it.”

Ada looked down. Kit had remarkably long shoes. She pulled up the stake.

They had met nine minutes earlier, when Leon, the volunteer coordinator, assigned them plot fourteen for the season. Now he was working his way down the row, handing out locker keys on rings made of bent curtain hooks. The garden had once been a bowling green. A wall shaded the east end, and on the sunny west edge of their plot a narrow path served both them and the woman who grew onions next door.

“My dad grew these up the whole side of his garage,” Ada said. She opened the tobacco tin in her pocket. Inside were forty or fifty beans, almost black, each with a little cream dash that looked painted on. “I was hoping for two rows.”

Kit tipped six packets of flower seed out of a coat pocket. “My brother’s getting married in August. I told him I’d do the tables.”

“How many?”

“I asked him that after I said yes. Twelve.”

Leon came back with their key. There was one locker for each plot and one key for each locker. He did his rounds the first Wednesday of every month, he told them. If they wanted help in between, his number was on the shed.

“Which of you wants to keep this?”

“Kit,” Ada said, at the same time Kit said, “Ada.”

“I can only come Wednesday evenings,” Kit explained. “I sew alterations at the department store. Wednesday we close at five, and Sunday I have my brother’s kids. If there’s a nice Sunday in June you’ll be standing here wanting a hoe and I’ll be watching a seven-year-old swallow pool water.”

Ada took the key. “I’m usually home by six.”

“Usually is what worries me.”

They stood over the bare ground. Ada had imagined kneeling here alone, dropping the beans into holes the depth of her first knuckle, as her father had shown her when they were both young enough to get down on their knees without thinking about it. This person in the long shoes had imagined twelve tables.

“Across the north end,” Kit said. “Could you put the beans there? The sun would still get to the flowers. I could leave a gap through the middle so you could get to them.”

Ada looked at the length of the bed. “If you give me three feet at that end, I can make a tent of the poles. You could plant right up to it on the south side.”

Kit measured three feet with a yellow dressmaker’s tape. Ada set the stake again.

They sowed two kinds of zinnia and some cosmos, putting the other packets away for another year. Ada planted sixteen beans. While Kit fetched water she found herself counting them again, as if somebody might later require an accounting for the ones she had left in the tin.

At the gate she showed Kit the little grocery opposite, where the owner let her leave things for her daughter. They went in to ask if he would hold their key on Wednesdays. He considered the curtain hook.

“In an envelope,” he said. “Last thing I need is another object nobody knows the purpose of.”

Kit borrowed his pen and wrote PLOT FOURTEEN in the square, clear capitals of someone accustomed to chalking marks on expensive cloth.

* * *

On the first Wednesday in July, Ada arrived carrying string and found a bicycle lying in the onions. Its owner, Leon’s ten-year-old daughter, stood beside it with a green smear down one shin. The onion grower was straightening the bent tops, and Kit was unwinding a bean runner from a bicycle pedal.

“You all right?” Ada asked the girl.

“I was walking it,” she said fiercely.

“She was,” Kit said. “The pedal caught.”

Since May the bean tent had become a house. Runners went up the poles, met at the top, and came down again with energy to spare. The cosmos along the path had also done well. Together they had narrowed the passage to a slot you could negotiate sideways, provided nothing about you stuck out.

Leon took hold of the handlebars. “I need a wheelbarrow to get through here by the time I finish my rounds.”

Ada lifted a hanging runner so the girl could wheel her bike away. Under its leaves dangled the first beans long enough to pick. Her father used to send her home with a bread bag full, whether she wanted them or not. She had been looking forward to being the person who did the sending.

“I can tie these back,” she said.

“You tied them back last week.” Kit wiped bicycle grease onto a scrap of lining silk. “I’ll take out this row of cosmos. Can you move that end pole in?”

“There are four plants on it.”

“And there are flowers on mine.”

They regarded the path. From the grocery across the road came the clank of the shutters being raised for evening trade.

“I watered those flowers through eleven days without rain,” Ada said. Hearing it aloud, she realized she had been saving it up.

Kit sat back on their heels. “I know how often you’re here. Every time I pick up the key he tells me.”

“I didn’t ask him to.”

“I know that too.” Kit folded the silk over the greasy spot. “I thought we’d agreed you liked watering. You said it was the best part of your day.”

“It was May when I said that.”

A laugh got out of Kit, and after a moment Ada joined in. It had been a beautiful May. You could have got her to agree to anything.

Kit took the tape from around their neck and measured the width of the path farther down. They drew a line in the dirt with the point of the sewing scissors. “If we clear up to here, he’ll get his barrow through. What about those bean plants that run up together? Could we lead the tops across to the next pole before we shift this one?”

Ada felt along the stems. Two were still supple. The other two had wound themselves together so tightly she could barely get a finger between them.

“Give me the scissors,” she said.

She cut the two thick stems low. Kit worked the severed vines loose while Ada eased the remaining pair, still attached to their pole, toward the middle of the tent. Then they lifted six cosmos with slabs of earth around their roots. The onion grower took three for her front steps. The others went in the gap through the middle of the plot, which Ada decided she could give up now that she could reach the beans from the path.

Leon came back pushing a barrow with his daughter sitting in it like a queen. She held her arms tight against her sides as they passed. A cosmos leaf brushed her elbow. Nothing broke.

“I can soak everything properly on Wednesdays,” Kit said. “And put down that leaf mulch Leon’s been trying to give away. Would twice a week besides that do it?”

“Most weeks. If we get another hot spell I’ll ring you and complain.”

“Please do. I spend all day with people who tell me a jacket fits beautifully and then bring it back.”

Before they left, Ada divided the beans from the cut vines into two small handfuls. Kit ate one raw and pronounced it furry. Ada told them to get a pan of water boiling first, for God’s sake.

* * *

On the third Wednesday in August, Kit came to cut the flowers wearing good gray trousers and carrying two white buckets from the store’s cleaning cupboard. Ada had unlocked the locker and put the snips out. The first zinnias were fading, but the plants they had cut back in July had branched, and now the bed was full of heads in shades of orange and rose. Two of the transplanted cosmos had lived. Their white flowers hung out over the zinnias on stems so fine they seemed to have arrived separately and settled there.

“Still twelve tables?” Ada asked.

“Eleven. My brother discovered he had seated our dead aunt. He’d worked from the invitation list for his first wedding.”

Ada counted out twelve bunches anyway. “One for the kitchen. Somebody always gets stuck in there.”

They cut in the long evening light, stripping the lower leaves so they wouldn’t foul the water. Halfway through, Kit stopped with the snips open.

“It looks a bit awful where we’ve been.”

“It does,” Ada said. A whole summer of coming through the gate and looking first for color, and now she could see the wall. “Keep going. You promised him flowers.”

Kit cut the last long zinnia, a pink one with a crooked neck that would hang nicely over the lip of a jug. Ada held the buckets steady while Kit fitted it in among the others.

Along the moved pole hung three dry pods Ada had saved for seed. She broke one open. The beans lay in their hollows, black with cream dashes, as clean as if her father had just rubbed them on his shirt.

Kit held out a hand. “Could I have a couple? My niece has decided the wedding flowers are hers to grow next year. I’d like something of my own.”

Ada tipped the whole pod into Kit’s palm. Kit found the key envelope in the locker and slipped the beans inside, folding the flap twice.

At the gate each took a bucket. Even so, water slopped onto Kit’s good trousers. They waited for a gap in traffic, then carried the flowers across the road together. Inside the grocery the owner moved his pyramid of oranges so they could set the buckets on his counter while Kit fetched the car.

“Which one’s mine?” he asked.

Ada looked at Kit. Kit lifted the crooked pink zinnia out of the wedding flowers and laid it across his till.
