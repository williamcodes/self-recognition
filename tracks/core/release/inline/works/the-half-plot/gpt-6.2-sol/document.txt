On the first Wednesday in May, Ada arrived at Plot 19 with six bean poles and found a stranger measuring it in shoes.

“Eleven and a half,” the stranger said, completing a heel-to-toe traverse of the bed. “I was promised twelve.”

“Feet?”

“Mine, apparently.”

Ada set down her poles. The stranger stepped off the soil and put out a hand. Their name was Kit. They had brought a paper bag from the hardware store and a drawing on the back of an envelope. The drawing showed three broad bands of flowers, with a little square at the bottom labeled BUCKET.

“My sister’s getting married in August,” Kit said. “I told her I could do the tables.”

“How many tables?”

“Six when I told her.”

Ada took a small brown envelope from her coat pocket. She had written the year on it in pencil. Inside were the last beans she’d shelled with her father at his kitchen table. He had made her pick through the pile twice because she kept letting the split ones go into the envelope. She’d found another split one that morning and put it back with the others. It had seemed a poor day to start throwing things away.

“I need room for these,” she said.

Maribel, the volunteer coordinator, came through the gate with a clipboard and a key on a green plastic coil. She was giving the new plot holders their May check. The strip of packed earth along the south edge, she told them, was their shared path. It had to stay clear from the gate to the water tap. She handed Ada the key, which opened the tool locker beside the gate.

“One per plot. I come round once a month, so put both your names on the board if you need me to call.”

Ada slipped the coil over her wrist. Kit looked at it.

“I can only come Wednesday evenings,” they said. “I close the bakery the other nights. Could you leave that somewhere I can get it?”

Ada had already imagined bringing the key home and hanging it on the hook where she kept her spare. She looked at the locker. Its feet stood on two cinder blocks. Kit crouched and reached into the back of one, up to the wrist.

“Can you see my hand?”

Ada bent down. “I can see your watch.”

Kit took off the watch and tried again. They agreed on the block.

With a stick, Ada drew a line down the middle of the bed, dividing it into two rectangles that each met the path. She wanted her bean poles at the front of hers. The path would give her somewhere firm to stand while she picked, and she could see the pods with the light behind them. Her father used to come in from picking with green crescents on his thumbnail.

Kit followed the line of the poles with their eyes. “How tall do those get?”

“As tall as the poles, then they look for something else.”

“Could we keep this corner open?” Kit pointed to the end nearest the tap. “I’ll be coming in with a bucket after work. I’d like to get it all the way into the bed.”

Ada shifted the line a shoe’s width at the front. Kit redrew the flower bands to fit and gave Ada the stick so she could mark the first bean hole. By the time the light went, Kit had shaken out the zinnia seeds and folded the empty packet into a square small enough to fit behind their watch. Ada planted nine beans and kept three in the envelope.

* * *

At the July check, Maribel laid her clipboard flat on the path. A bean runner had found the wire fence on the other side and crossed at about the height of her knee. She lifted it with the end of her pen.

“This comes down tonight,” she said. “Somebody’s going to carry a watering can straight through it.”

Ada fetched the scissors. She had tucked the runner back into the poles the previous weekend. When she cut it, the loose end stayed in the air a moment, hooked to itself, before falling.

Kit let themselves in just after seven, still wearing a white bakery shirt with a streak of something dark at the elbow. Ada could tell when they saw the gap in the flowers. They set their bag down before they said anything.

“Where are the cosmos?”

“By the tap.”

Three drooping plants stood in a row at the edge of the bucket space. Ada had moved them on Sunday. Their roots had held almost no soil, and she’d carried each plant across the bed with both hands cupped around a little rag of dirt.

“They were leaning into the beans,” she said. “They’d have snapped.”

Kit pressed one finger into the ground beside a stem. “I put stakes in the locker for them.”

Ada had seen the stakes. She had taken them for kindling left by the last plot holder.

Kit picked up the bucket and tried to set it beside the tap. There was room for the base if they tipped it, though the handle caught a stem. They lifted it out again. Ada could hear water running in the next plot, splashing against the hollow sides of somebody else’s can.

“I can move them back,” she said.

Kit glanced at the strip where the cosmos had been. The beans cast a shadow across it and over the first row of zinnias. Some of the zinnias had grown sideways toward the path. Kit slid a palm beneath one of the stems. A bud the size of a shirt button rested against their finger.

“I think the poles need to go to the back,” they said.

Ada looked up at the bean leaves, which were so thick now she could stand behind them and be quite alone. “The roots are at the front.”

“Can we leave the roots there?”

They stood for a while looking from the bases of the plants to the far corners of the bed. Kit took two of the thin stakes from the locker and held them out at an angle. Ada could see how the vines might go up that way, with a low section at the front and the height at the back. She put a hand around one of her poles and felt it give. Several runners wound around it together. One had already wound twice around its neighbor.

“We’d have to cut some,” she said.

Kit put the stakes down.

Ada found the stems she could follow cleanly from the ground to the first fork. She gave those to Kit to hold while she worked loose the twine. There were two she couldn’t free. She cut them low, with her thumb over the place where the blades met, then carried the poles to the back and pushed them in. They used Kit’s stakes to keep the vines off the soil on the way up. By the time they tied the last knot, Ada had dirt down the front of her shirt and Kit’s white sleeve was green.

Kit pinched a spent bloom from one of the cosmos and carried the plant to the sunny space the poles had vacated. They dug the other two holes wide enough to take a shovelful of earth with each plant. When the bucket fitted at the end again, Kit filled it and watered the transplants slowly. Ada held each stem upright while the ground settled around it.

“How many tables now?” Ada asked.

“Eight. My sister says the cousins can sit together, which is how we got nine last week.”

Ada washed the scissors under the tap. “Bring nine jars.”

* * *

On the Wednesday before the wedding, Kit came through the gate carrying a cardboard wine box with nine jars packed into it. Ada was sitting on the locker step, shelling a pod she’d left too long. She slid over so Kit could reach the cinder block. The green coil had bleached almost white on the side that stuck out into the sun.

At the back of the bed, the beans had reached the tops of the poles again. Ada had left a few pods to dry for seed. She picked the tender ones by reaching between the slanted stakes, and sometimes she had to get down on both knees. She had brought a shallow bowl for the evening’s picking. Kit looked into it and chose a bean with a curl at one end.

“Can I eat it like this?”

“That’s how I test them.”

Kit bit into it. Ada waited for them to say what it tasted like. Her father had always said the younger ones tasted of peas. She remembered disagreeing with him, though she could no longer remember what she’d thought they tasted of instead.

Kit finished the bean and opened the locker for the shears.

The zinnias in the sunny strip stood higher than Kit’s waist. They had bloomed in brick red and pale yellow and a pink that Kit’s sister had described, when shown a photograph, as exactly the color of the bathroom she’d spent two years trying to get her landlord to repaint. Kit had brought the photograph with that message still beneath it. Ada had laughed hard enough to have to sit down.

Now Kit filled the bucket at the tap, set it in its square, and took hold of the tallest pink stem. They followed it down to a pair of leaves. At the click of the shears, Ada put out her hand, and Kit gave her the flower. She stripped the leaves that would sit below the water and lowered the stem into the bucket. They worked down the row like that. Kit turned each stem to see where the next bud was coming, and Ada made room in the bucket. There were enough long stems for all the jars. Kit took some of the cosmos last, including a white one from the plant that had wilted worst in July. They left it a flower with a yellow center already busy with a bee.

When the light began to go, Kit set the jars along the path and divided the stems among them. The pink flowers went in every jar. The last one had a bend halfway up that put its bloom close to the glass.

“That one’s my sister’s,” Kit said, and fitted the jar into the middle of the box.

Ada waited while Kit took the shears back. She heard the locker shut and the little scrape of the key going into the block.

Kit lifted the box. The flowers brushed their chin. Ada walked ahead to hold the gate, with the bowl of beans tucked against her hip. At the sidewalk Kit stopped to shift their grip, and Ada steadied the box from underneath until they had it. Then Kit carried the flowers out into the last of the light.
