The spring concert programs came off the copier with a gray stripe down the middle of every daffodil. I folded forty of them before I noticed that our piece, “Three Boats,” had no names underneath it except the composer's. This was lucky, I thought, because if everything went the way I hoped, somebody would have to change what it said beside mine.

Then Nell came through the band room door wheeling a suitcase.

One of its wheels squeaked each time it went around. We all watched her roll it the whole length of the room.

“Your flight was tomorrow,” I said.

“Hello to you, too.”

Nell had gone to Portugal for her grandparents' fiftieth anniversary. She'd been away nine days. The first time she'd missed rehearsal, Mr. Vale had handed me a copy of her first clarinet part and said, “We may need you up here, Ivo. We'll try it on concert day.”

For six weeks I'd played second. For nine days I'd played second at school, then gone home and played first to the coats hanging in our hall. Our flat was small enough that the music stand blocked the bathroom door. My little brother had learned to crawl under it.

Nell set her case on a chair. “Dad got us on an earlier flight. I slept through breakfast and a whole ocean.”

Mr. Vale came in carrying four sheets of music still warm from the printer. He took in Nell, the suitcase, and me sitting in Nell's chair.

“Good,” he said. “We can actually try both.”

He set the sheets down. Across the top of each he'd penciled *Passing version*. “The quartet gets twenty minutes. After that, the choir needs this room and I need an actual sandwich. You four decide what goes onstage.”

I looked at the clock. Four ten.

“Do I still get to try first?”

“Yes,” said Mr. Vale, and Nell took the chair beside mine.

In the original arrangement of “Three Boats,” the cello played a rocking figure, low note, high note, low. The viola put two short strokes against it. Second clarinet came in underneath first, mostly in thirds, the way two people on different stairs can climb at the same speed. First clarinet got the tune. It started with one long note, then four quick ones that curled back to where they'd come from.

I knew how Nell played that curl. She leaned on the first of the quick notes and let the other three tumble. I'd worked out a way to make all four as even as drops from a tap.

We played the original with me on first. The long note came out beautifully. I could feel it in my front teeth. My fingers did exactly what I'd trained them to do in the hall. When the tune climbed, Nell climbed with me, and for once I was the person who got to decide how fast the stairs went by.

At the end, Priya lifted her cello bow. “That sounded expensive.”

“What does expensive sound like?” asked Gus, our violist.

“Like they should charge my dad to get in.”

I had to look down at my shoes for a second. “Can we do the last eight bars again?”

“Other version first,” said Nell. “Clock.”

Four sixteen already.

In the passing version, all four of us got one turn with the opening tune. Nell started, then Gus took it down an octave. Priya had it next, high on the cello, and I brought it back up on clarinet. While somebody else had the tune, my part was mostly the same second part I'd practiced for six weeks. Mostly. At the bottom of the first page, where my old part had two bars of rest, the new one gave me three little repeated notes to lead into Gus's entrance.

We made it as far as Priya. Then there was a sound like somebody shaking a drawer of cutlery, and we stopped.

“I can't hear where I come in,” she said.

“I gave you a nod,” said Gus.

“You gave your left knee a nod. Your left knee and I are practically strangers.”

Nell laughed so hard she had to take the reed out of her mouth. I checked the time. Four twenty-one. We could play the original twice more if everybody would quit being hilarious.

“Look,” I said. “We have one that works.”

Gus was turning his bow in his fingers. “Can I hear what mine's supposed to sound like first? I've been going *chik chik* since February.”

He played his four bars alone. On viola the quick notes had a little grain to them, like the noise a pencil makes shading sideways. At the end, his bow kept moving long after I'd expected him to run out. Priya watched it slow. This time she came in exactly right.

“Oh,” she said. “I can see that.”

“Let's go from my three notes,” I said.

My first try, I played them as three identical little pecks. Gus came in sounding like a fourth peck. My second try, I let each note grow a bit, until the third one almost asked a question. Gus swept straight out of it. Priya's answer sounded different again. She slid her finger just enough on the climb that the cello seemed to take the stairs two at a time.

Then came my turn. I played my four quick drops, exactly even. In the space after them I heard Nell take a breath to join me for the ending.

Four twenty-seven.

“I still like the original,” I said.

“I like you playing it,” said Nell. “You've got the high bit much cleaner than me. I like this one better, though.”

Mr. Vale was at his desk unwrapping his sandwich. He had a slice of tomato balanced on one thumb. Clearly we were on our own.

“Passing,” said Priya.

“Passing,” said Gus.

They waited. I wanted that long opening note with the whole auditorium quiet around it. I'd already pictured my brother recognizing the tune from the hallway and whispering to Mum.

“Passing,” I said finally. “But let me play the opening on my own sometime. At the summer assembly or something.”

“Ask him now,” said Nell.

Mr. Vale put the tomato back in his sandwich. “Come see me Monday. Bring the music.”

Then the choir came in, thirty people all going *mee may mah moh moo* at different speeds, and our twenty minutes were up.

During the break we ate rolls on the steps outside the auditorium. Nell had a foil packet of almonds from the plane. She picked out the whole ones and gave me the bits, which sounds mean unless you know I like the salt at the bottom.

“Were you hoping I'd miss the concert?” she asked.

I considered lying. “I was hoping you'd turn up about halfway through.”

“That's very specific.”

“I didn't want you stranded in Portugal. I wanted four minutes on clarinet.”

She shook some more almond bits into my palm. Through the doors we could hear the junior choir singing something about a blackbird. My brother appeared in his good shirt with the bottom button in the wrong hole.

“Are you doing the coat song?” he asked.

“Fourth time you hear it, that's me.”

He held up four fingers. Mum called him back before he could ask why.

When we walked onstage, the lights turned the audience into a few pale faces floating above a dark sea of jumpers. I could see my brother because he was still holding his hand up and Mum was trying to get him to put it down.

We tuned. In rehearsal I'd always listened for the moment my note lined up with Nell's and the little wobble between us disappeared. This time I could hear Gus turning a peg in tiny creaks. Priya rested her bow against the string. The cello began to rock.

Nell's opening note filled the hall. On the four quick ones she did that familiar tumble. Underneath her, my second part fitted so easily I could look up from the stand. Gus had tucked his left foot behind his right. His bow was poised halfway along the string.

My three notes came. I made the first small, the next a little bigger. On the third, Gus's elbow rose and the sound changed color right in front of me. From the seats it might have sounded like one instrument doing an impossible thing.

In the back row somebody dropped a program. Six weeks ago that would have been the only thing I'd heard. Now I was watching the last inch of Gus's bow. So was Priya. She caught his note and carried it up the cello string, and I heard, for the first time, what Nell was doing under her. Two short notes. *Chik chik.* Nell, who usually got the tune, was being Gus.

I nearly smiled myself off my reed.

Priya reached the top of her climb. Usually this was where, even while other people played, I started singing my own part inside my head. There wasn't room this time. I wanted to hear how long she'd stay up there.

She let the note hang for one lovely, dangerous instant. Then her bow eased and I took my breath with it.

My long note felt different coming out of hers. I let the first quick note stretch a hair. The next three went tumbling after it, just as Nell had played them at the start. Beside me I heard her second clarinet line rise to meet mine. We went up the last stairs together.

After the applause, as we bent to pick up our music, I saw my brother in the second row. He'd folded his striped daffodil program into a narrow tube. He put it to his mouth like a clarinet and wiggled his fingers at me.

Out in the corridor he played me what he'd heard. I expected the coat song. Instead he sang my three repeated notes into the end of the paper tube, each a little louder than the last.

Then he pointed it at me. He was waiting for the viola.
