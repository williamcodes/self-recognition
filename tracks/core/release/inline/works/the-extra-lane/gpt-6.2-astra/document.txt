On his last Tuesday before moving to Toledo, Art took his bowling ball, his shoes, and the little electric fan he had been bringing to Comet Lanes for nineteen years. He left the fan’s extension cord. For the better part of the next Tuesday, the members of the Extension Cords considered this funny enough to make up for losing him.

Then they saw his replacement bowl.

Leon Price walked up, swung a handsome old black ball, and put it in the gutter. Across the pair of lanes, a woman on the rival Night Owls covered her face with her hands.

“Give her a minute,” Leon said. “Doris has been my best friend since 1968. She’s seen all my gutter balls and she thinks there’s a lifetime limit.”

Priya penciled a dash in the first little box on Leon’s score sheet. He had brought the sheet himself, ruled on cream paper. All the other scores glowed overhead in purple.

“Nice dash,” he said.

“Thank you. I went to school for it.”

“Architecture?”

“Pharmacy. We do a lot of dashes.”

Leon got eight pins with his second ball. Priya entered the eight. Ten pins stood at the beginning of each frame, and ordinarily you got two balls to knock them down. Nobody had to understand much more than that to appreciate an eight after a gutter ball, and the Cords clapped him back to his seat.

Priya had been captain for three weeks. She was twenty-six. The next youngest Cord, her auntie Val, was fifty-three and could remember putting Priya into a onesie printed with little ducks. In the first week, Priya had asked everybody to arrive ten minutes early. They had all admired the idea from home. In the second, she had changed the order of play, and Walt had bowled first anyway because his shoes were already tied.

This week she had recruited Leon from the breakfast counter, where he drew the specials on the chalkboard in exchange for eggs. She had seen him bowl exactly once. He had been decent. What had sold her was watching him put a curl on the dollar sign in TWO EGGS $3 and then lean back to enjoy it.

Halfway through the game, the pinsetter on lane six developed a tendency to leave the back corner pin lying down. Priya went to the desk to get it fixed. By the time she came back, Walt had asked Doris to come over and give Leon a lesson.

“There’s a thumb thing he used to do,” Doris explained. “He knows the thing.”

“I might know several thumb things by now,” Leon said.

“He’s up,” Priya said.

“I’ll only take a minute.”

“Doris. He’s up.”

Doris returned to the Owls. Leon hurried his approach and missed four pins he might have had. The Cords lost by thirty-one. As the teams packed up, Priya found him at the breakfast counter touching up the chalk letters in TOMATO SOUP.

“I sounded like his mother,” she said, nodding toward Walt.

“What did his mother sound like?”

“No idea.”

“Then you can stop worrying. Here. Fix that O.”

Priya took the chalk. The second O in TOMATO was too narrow. She widened it on the left, where there was room.

“You see space,” Leon said with satisfaction. “Lots of people only see what’s in it.”

“Did you mind me cutting Doris off?”

“I minded all three of you discussing my thumb while I was standing there attached to it.”

Priya turned the chalk around in her fingers. “Saturday practice. Want to help me run it?”

“What does that entail?”

“Mostly reminding me you’re attached to your thumb.”

“That I can do.”

***

Saturday morning the bowling alley smelled of coffee and wet wool. Val arrived first. Walt showed up ten minutes late with a box of powdered doughnuts, the tops of which bore the impression of his bowling bag.

Priya had set out four score sheets. “Today everybody scores for somebody else,” she said. “And if you want to give advice, ask first.”

“What if it’s really good advice?” said Val.

“Imagine the joy of being asked twice.”

Leon laughed through a mouthful of doughnut. A small white cloud escaped his nose, which pleased Priya more than it ought to have.

He showed them how he drew a spare, one clean slash for knocking down the last pins with your second ball. A spare earned ten plus whatever you knocked down on your next ball, so you left the total blank until then. A strike, all ten with the first ball, went down as an X and earned the next two balls as a bonus.

“Like the pharmacy calling to say the rest of your order will be ready tomorrow,” Priya said.

“You’ve been to my pharmacy,” said Walt.

They played for an hour. Keeping Val’s score, Walt discovered that her three quiet spares had earned her more than his one thunderous strike and the assorted wreckage thereafter. Keeping Leon’s, Priya noticed that he was good at aiming for one particular pin. It was the big triangular crowd of them that gave him trouble.

“Sign painter,” he said when she mentioned it. “Tell me where to end a line and I can generally get there.”

She held her pencil over his sheet. “Want something to try?”

“I do.”

“Pick one arrow on the lane. End the line there. Let the pins be somebody else’s department.”

His next ball slid over the second arrow and took down nine. He turned to her with such a look of private delight that she knew exactly how he must have looked at seven, getting away with something.

At the end, Priya tried a new order. Walt, Val, Priya, Leon.

“I generally go last,” Val said.

“You generally spend the last frame asking how many we need. I’d like you to have one whole Tuesday of peace.”

“And what about my peace?” Leon asked.

“You brought your own arithmetic.”

He considered this fair.

While Walt and Val got their coats, Leon and Priya drew imaginary businesses on the backs of the used sheets. Priya invented a restaurant called The Assorted Wreckage. Leon gave it a listing neon cocktail glass. By the time Doris came in for her Saturday game, they were debating whether a pharmacy called Side Effects could stay in business on the strength of its beautiful sign.

Doris looked over Leon’s shoulder. “He used to paint windows backward,” she told Priya. “On the inside of the glass so the weather couldn’t get at the gold. You ever see anybody write an ampersand backward?”

“Come on Tuesday,” Priya said to Leon. “I want an ampersand.”

“I thought you wanted a hundred and forty.”

“I contain multitudes.”

***

The rematch drew an audience of six, including the man who maintained the pinsetters, who was eating a pear. Doris had brought the extension cord back from underneath the Owls’ bench, where somebody had kicked it the week before. Val coiled it and hung it off the back of Art’s old chair.

Walt, though his shoes were tied a full twelve minutes early, waited for Priya to hand him the ball.

It was a close game. Val made a spare so delicate that the last pin seemed simply to reconsider its position. Walt asked Leon whether he wanted a tip, received a courteous no, and went off to buy some licorice. Priya missed an easy spare in the ninth and let herself feel sick about it for exactly the time it took to sharpen her pencil.

In the tenth and final frame Doris finished first, with a spare. A spare in the tenth gets an extra ball right away to settle the bonus. She got seven on hers, bowed to the pinsetter man, and sat down. Now every Owl was done. Only Leon had to bowl.

Priya added the figures on the cream sheet. The Cords were nine pins behind. Ten would win.

“Want the number?” she asked.

“Yes, please.”

“Ten. You can take two balls to get there.”

Leon went up and rolled. Eight fell. The two left standing were the far back corners, one on either side of the lane. The pinsetter man lowered his pear.

From the Owls’ bench Doris made a tiny motion as if sliding a visiting card across a table. Leon knew it well. Clip the outside edge of one pin and send it skidding sideways into the other. Together they had tried it a hundred times. Once, during the first Bush administration, it had worked.

“Captain,” Leon said. “How do you feel about a draw?”

Priya looked at the two pins. Get one and they would tie. Try for both and there was a wide, clean gutter waiting for the difference between a miracle and a perfectly respectable miss.

“I’d take a draw,” she said. Then she put down the pencil. “What would you like?”

“I’d like that left pin. Been looking at it all evening.”

“Then get me that particular pin.”

Leon picked his arrow. His ball crossed it, went on down the lane and took the left pin squarely in the belly. The right one stood untouched. On the purple screen the two totals came up together, 587 and 587.

For a second everybody sat still. Then Val said, with feeling, “God, it’s good going second,” and both benches broke up.

Doris came over and bumped shoulders with Leon. “In 1991 you’d have tried it.”

“In 1991 you owed me forty dollars and I still had hopes of collecting.”

Priya was filling in the last box when Walt leaned over. “Same order next week, Captain?”

She considered making him wait. She liked, too much perhaps, the sound of the question. “Same order.”

Leon borrowed her pencil. Beneath the two scores he wrote EXTENSION CORDS in shaded capitals, turned the paper over, and drew an ampersand backward. When she turned it faceup again and held it to the purple light, the ampersand sat exactly where it belonged. He finished with NIGHT OWLS.

“Leave me a little room,” Priya said.

In the space beneath the lettering she drew a small electric fan, its cord running off the edge of the paper. They taped the sheet inside the glass door for Art, who would see it backward from Toledo, and for everybody coming in, who would see it just fine.
