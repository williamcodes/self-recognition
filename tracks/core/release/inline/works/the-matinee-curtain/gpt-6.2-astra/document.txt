At the final rehearsal, Sal sold the same woman a spool of blue thread six times. On the seventh, Evelyn said, “Could you possibly let go of it?”

Sal looked down. They each had two fingers on the spool.

“Sorry,” Sal said.

“Save it up,” called Ruth from the middle of the auditorium. “You’ve only got four lines and that isn’t one.”

This got the laugh Ruth had meant it to get. Sal let go. The spool dropped and rolled under the counter, where the real shopkeeper, if she had any sense, would have left it.

From the wings Jo pushed another spool through the little hatch Sal had built for exactly this contingency. Evelyn took it. The scene went on.

For twenty years Sal had supplied the Bell Street Players with things that could pass through a hatch. They had made roast chickens that would bounce, love letters in six different hands, and a wedding cake with one edible corner. When Ruth announced *The Street of Widows*, Sal had volunteered to play Mrs. Vale, who sold sewing notions. They wanted to stand once inside a room they had made and have somebody come in the door.

“Saturday night and Sunday afternoon,” Ruth had promised. “You’ll do both.”

Jo, seventeen, was the understudy. She had come in originally to record the building. She wanted to make sound for radio plays, an occupation Sal had believed went out with their father’s teeth in a glass. For three weeks she had been going around in headphones, tapping radiators. She had a recording of the ticket window going up that she claimed sounded precisely like a guillotine. Sal wondered about her sources.

They had practiced Mrs. Vale’s scene while laying out its properties. Scissors closed, handles downstage. Three cards of buttons, red on top. The spool on its side so Evelyn, whose hands shook, could scoop it up. Jo played both parts until Sal learned to hear the difference between a silence Evelyn meant to leave and one that needed filling.

After rehearsal Jo came through the shop door carrying her recorder. “I like how you do the last line.”

“Because then we’re done?”

“Because you turn away. Like you’d be embarrassed to see if it helped.”

Sal began shutting the drawers. Each had its contents drawn in pencil on the bottom so anybody striking the set could tell what was missing. It had never occurred to them to draw what went on top of the counter. Somebody had put the scissors in a coffee mug again.

“My aunt and my grandpa are coming Sunday,” Jo said. “She traded a shift so she could drive him. It’s the only one.”

Sal took the scissors out of the mug.

“I told them what an understudy is,” Jo added.

“Have they ever seen you act?”

“My grandpa saw me be a flood in fourth grade. It was mostly fabric.”

At home Sal stood at the sink and said the four lines without moving their lips. In twenty years nobody had come to see a chicken.

Saturday at half hour they signed in on the clipboard by the stage door, then went from habit to check the property tables. Jo had already set them. The honey jar held cold tea. The sugar jar held sugar, following a celebrated dispute about what else an actor might agree to put in his mouth. Under the work light the gunmetal scissors lay precisely within their white tape outline.

Sal sat while Jo did up the tiny boot buttons with a bent crochet hook. Out front the audience made the low continuous noise that, heard for twenty years through a wall, had always sounded friendly. Tonight it sounded like people who had bought something.

“Want to run it?” Jo said.

“I’d rather hear about the flood.”

“We drowned three farmers and settled into a lake.”

Then the house lights went down.

Sal spent the first act in the passage with a shawl over their knees. When the shop rolled into place, they helped set the brake until the stage manager slapped their hand very softly. They went round to the front, stood behind the counter, and felt a draft come up through the little hatch.

Evelyn entered in a green coat with the collar gone shiny.

“Morning, Annie,” Sal said.

Nobody laughed. They had somehow expected somebody to laugh at the fact of them speaking. Evelyn asked for the blue thread. Her boy was coming home and his good jacket needed hemming.

“That one’s French,” Sal said.

Evelyn asked how much.

“For you, a penny.”

The spool went neatly from hand to hand. Out on the street the telegram boy passed the window on his bicycle. Everybody in the theater knew where he was going, except the woman in the green coat.

“I’ll put it on your bill,” Sal said, and turned to the drawers.

In the dressing room afterward they found they could recall the whole scene with an almost physical delight, particularly the second or two before Evelyn entered, when the shop had been theirs to keep.

Sunday morning Sal called Ruth.

“I want Jo to do it today,” they said. Saying it took some of the pleasure out of having decided it.

There was a clink of spoon against china. “Do you actually want that?”

“They can only come today.”

“I know. I sold her the tickets.”

Sal’s kitchen window faced the back of the theater across two narrow yards. Somebody had propped the stage door open with the concrete pineapple Sal had cast for *Cabaret*. It was a bad doorstop. It rolled.

“I want my second show,” they said.

“Then I’m glad you told me. You might try telling Jo.”

The pineapple rolled. The door went shut on somebody’s armload of towels.

At noon Jo was beneath the shop counter, listening to something. Sal sat on the little stool and waited until her headphones emerged.

“I considered giving you the part today,” they said. “I’m going to play it. I thought I ought to say so to your face.”

“All right.” Jo wound the cord around her hand. “Did you think I was asking?”

“I couldn’t tell.”

“I sort of was. I figured if you offered, I’d say yes.” She tucked the recorder away. “I can want to do it without you having to ask my permission to do it yourself.”

“That sounds like something you worked out in advance.”

“I was awake a long time.”

They sat awhile on opposite sides of the counter. In the hall somebody was shaking yesterday’s crumbs out of the rain machine. Sal could tell by the delicious sound of lentils hitting the floor.

“What did you come under here to get?” they asked.

“The bicycle. From the house it sounds like a kid riding a bike. From down here you can hear him trying to make it up the little ramp. You can hear what it costs him.”

Jo had been making a three-minute piece for a summer radio workshop. Sal had assumed all her knocking and tapping would eventually go into separate files labeled with names like DOOR. In fact she was telling the play from the telegram boy’s point of view. He had no lines at all.

“Does it end at the house where he delivers it?” Sal asked.

“He has to take the bike back. I think that’s probably the worst part.”

At half hour Sal watched Jo take her relatives to their seats. Her aunt had on a peach-colored work shirt with a darker rectangle where she’d removed her name tag. Her grandfather carried his program folded to the page where Jo’s name appeared in small type. He asked her something and she pointed up to the booth, then down to the apron, then made a little rolling motion with one hand. He bent his head close to hers. Sal went back to get their boots buttoned.

The second time, what surprised them was how much they could see. Evelyn had mended the cuff of the green coat herself with thread a shade too light. The boy on the bicycle had a pink place on his neck where his collar rubbed. When Evelyn reached for the spool it slipped past her fingers and fell into the open drawer.

Sal could have taken the spare from Jo. Instead they drew the drawer out a little farther. Evelyn looked in. For a second they were two people in a shop considering an ordinary nuisance. Then Evelyn gave a small snort through her nose and picked up the thread.

“I’ll put it on your bill,” Sal said.

This time they turned away because Annie had begun to weep and a woman ought to be able to get herself out of a shop with her purchase before anyone saw.

In the blackout Sal stood still until the brake came off and the shop began its backward journey with them aboard. Down below, two red lights on Jo’s recorder glowed in the dark. The telegram boy walked his bicycle back along the boards. For the first time Sal heard the loose link in the chain. It clicked once with every turn, at longer and longer intervals as he slowed to set the bicycle against the wall. Jo kept recording through the silence afterward.

For the curtain call they lined up by height in the passage. Sal could feel the hollow the crochet hook had left against their ankle. Across from them Jo was already gathering the things that might go missing, the scissors first, then the two blue spools, one warm from Evelyn’s hand.

The curtain lifted. Sal went out with the boy who rode the bicycle. He had been told to take their hand and he did, formally, as if helping them across a patch of ice. In the third row the man with the folded program was applauding. His niece leaned over to tell him something, and he nodded, still clapping, his face turned toward the strip of darkness beside the shop where Jo stood listening.

Sal bowed. As they straightened, the boy squeezed their fingers in his excitement, and they squeezed back.
