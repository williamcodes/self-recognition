# Reading the timetable together

## Pilot proposal

The museum should offer a one-term weekday pilot built around a question pupils aged nine to eleven can answer for themselves by the end of a visit. “If we have to be at the market by 10 o’clock, which service should we catch?” A paper timetable gives pupils a reason to look closely at a transport object, a problem they can solve together, and a way to check their own reasoning. The pilot would also introduce a simple booking reminder and an easy way for teachers to release or rearrange a date. These changes address two different opportunities in the museum’s figures.

## What the last three terms show

| Measure | Term 1 | Term 2 | Term 3 | Overall |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Classes booked | 12 | 16 | 14 | 42 |
| Classes attended | 10 | 13 | 12 | 35 |
| Share of booked classes attending | 83.3% | 81.3% | 85.7% | 83.3% |
| Correct pupil exit responses | 96 of 160 | 143 of 220 | 126 of 200 | 365 of 580 |
| Share of responses correct | 60.0% | 65.0% | 63.0% | 62.9% |

Term 2 brought the most classes through the doors despite having the lowest attendance rate. Term 3 converted the largest share of bookings into visits, although only one more class attended in Term 2 than in Term 3. Across all three terms, seven booked classes did not attend. The records give no reason for those missed visits. A reminder that also invites teachers to flag a transport or scheduling difficulty would help the museum learn which problems it can address.

Learning results rose five percentage points from Term 1 to Term 2 and fell two points in Term 3. Across the three terms, 215 of 580 survey respondents did not correctly describe how a paper timetable works. The survey counts tell us about respondents only. The museum has neither an arrival measure of what those pupils already knew nor a count of all attending pupils against which to check survey coverage. The pilot should preserve the exit question for comparison and add a practical test of whether a pupil can use a timetable independently.

## The 75-minute visit

Each class of up to 30 begins and ends together in Room A. For the middle of the visit, two groups of up to 15 exchange rooms. The educator stays with the timetable activity in Room A. The volunteer runs a rehearsed activity in Room B with a script and answer cards. The teacher can support pupils who need a familiar adult. The sequence requires no third museum worker.

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–8 | **Welcome and first attempt, Room A.** Give each pupil a folded response card. On the front, pupils use a small timetable to find the arrival time of one named service. Collect first attempts before discussing answers. Introduce the market journey and ask what a passenger would need to know before setting out. |
| 8–15 | **Read one journey together.** Display an enlarged timetable with stops down the side and one service in each column. The educator points to the departure stop, chooses a service and traces that column to the destination. A pupil repeats the trace while the class checks that both times belong to the same service. |
| 15–33 | **First rotation.** Half the class works with the educator in Room A and half with the volunteer in Room B. |
| 33–36 | **Change rooms.** Pupils leave the materials set out for the next group. The adults direct the exchange and give each group its new journey brief. |
| 36–54 | **Second rotation.** Each group completes the other activity. |
| 54–65 | **Make the market connection, Room A.** Reunite the class. In pairs, pupils choose the latest service that reaches the market by 10 o’clock and prepare to show their evidence. Invite two pairs to trace their choices. Ask whether an earlier service would also work and what the passenger would gain or lose by taking it. |
| 65–75 | **Independent exit and departure.** Give pupils a fresh timetable problem of the same difficulty as the first attempt, followed by the museum’s existing exit question. Pupils may point or explain orally if that is their usual way of responding. Reserve the final two minutes to collect cards and thank the class. |

In **Room A**, pairs act as passenger and booking clerk. The passenger draws a request such as “I’m leaving School Lane after 09:10 and need to reach Market Hall by 10:00.” The clerk places two counters on the timetable and reads back the stop and time under each. The passenger checks the journey before accepting a reusable ticket. Pupils swap roles for a second request. The educator watches for a revealing mistake, choosing a departure in one column and an arrival in the next, and asks the pair to trace the service between their counters.

In **Room B**, the volunteer gives each small group a reproduction of a collection timetable or a photograph of one, alongside a clearly labelled teaching copy. Pupils look for clues a passenger would have used, including the direction of travel, the stop names and the days of operation. They then test a fictional passenger’s claim against the teaching copy. One passenger, for example, has promised to arrive at the time printed for the stop *before* Market Hall. Pupils identify the mistake and correct the message. The volunteer’s answer card shows the route a finger should trace, so a pupil who gives an unexpected answer can demonstrate how they reached it.

## Materials and teacher preparation

Make one large demonstration timetable and 16 desk copies, enough for 15 pairs and a spare. Use the same fictional route throughout the teaching activities, with familiar stop names and a small route map. Print stop names in full, align the time columns, and offer large-print copies and card reading strips that expose one service at a time. Mark directions in words and with arrows so colour is optional. Prepare two sets of passenger requests, reusable tickets, counters, pencils, and separate entry and exit sheets with previously unseen times. Keep the historical reproduction clearly identified, with its date and route where known.

Send teachers a single preparation sheet with the booking confirmation. Fill in the meeting point, arrival time and contact details before sending it. The following text forms the body of the sheet.

> **Your class visit — Can we reach the market by ten?**
>
> During this 75-minute indoor visit, pupils will use a paper timetable to choose a service and explain their choice. Before the visit, spend five minutes asking where pupils have seen times displayed for passengers. Practise saying “ten minutes past nine” and finding its written form, 09:10. We will teach the timetable activity at the museum.
>
> Please divide the class into two groups of up to 15 and tell us the expected pupil count. Let us know if large print, reading support, a seated activity or another adjustment would help anyone take part. All activity materials will be waiting in the rooms.
>
> We will send a reminder one week before your visit. Please reply to confirm your numbers or tell us if your travel arrangements have changed. If you need another date, contact us as soon as you can so we can offer your place to another class.
>
> Back at school, ask pairs to write a new passenger request for a classmate. Have the passenger check both the departure and arrival times before accepting the answer.

## Pilot budget and evaluation

Offer 16 weekday class slots over one term, matching the highest previous booking total. The proposed **$2,700** covers $650 for educator planning, volunteer rehearsal and preparation time, $450 for timetable design and accessible versions, $400 for printing and durable station materials, $250 for consumables and replacement copies, $450 for booking coordination and teacher follow-up, and $500 for scoring, analysis and reporting. These are planning allowances. The museum should confirm costs before ordering and reuse the station kits across all visits.

Aim for **at least 14 classes to attend and at least 85% of booked classes to complete a visit**. Record each class once, including a class that rearranges within the term. Log dates offered, bookings, confirmations, cancellations, rearrangements and completed visits. Note reasons teachers volunteer and whether a released slot was filled. Report both the number of classes reached and the booking-to-attendance rate. With 16 bookings, a single class changes the rate by more than six percentage points.

Aim for **at least 75% of exit respondents to describe the timetable correctly**, compared with the historical 62.9%. Retain the earlier wording and scoring rule if available, and document any change. For the practical task, use matching anonymous codes on entry and exit sheets. Alternate which of two equivalent problems comes first across classes. Report the share of matched pupils answering correctly at each point and aim for a rise of at least 15 percentage points. Record how many pupils attended, how many answered each question and how many responses could be matched.

After each visit, the educator and volunteer should note where pupils lost their place and whether both groups finished in time. At term’s end, review these notes alongside the class-level results. If pupils can describe a timetable but still mix columns, revise the tracing activity. If classes learn successfully but bookings go unused, use teachers’ recorded reasons to adjust scheduling or reminders. The decision to repeat the pilot should weigh how many classes it reached, what pupils could do independently, and whether two adults could deliver the visit within 75 minutes.
