At nine on Sunday, Ellis was already outside the furniture exchange with two coffees balanced on the windowsill. He moved aside to let Tamsin unlock the door. Three Sundays ago he would have had it open by now, with the radio on and the sign out by the bus stop.

“Yours has milk,” he said.

Tamsin pushed the door with her hip. The new key caught unless she did that. Ellis had shown her on her first Sunday, then reached past her to turn it himself. This morning he picked up both coffees and waited.

The exchange occupied a former carpet shop. The shop floor was full, including the stretch of wall where the radiator had been taken out. The only space left for incoming furniture was a rectangle at the back, outlined in yellow tape. It was wide enough for a folding table with somebody standing behind it, or deep enough for six dining chairs in two rows. Tamsin had measured it on Thursday, using a tape measure that snapped shut hard enough to draw blood.

June came in carrying a bag of clean rags and went straight to the rectangle.

“Did she say what time?” she asked.

“Before noon. She’s bringing it in her van.”

“I brought the pillowcases.” June held up the bag. “For keeping the sets together.”

Last Sunday, June had sorted a donation of bedding on a sheet spread on the floor. At closing, she had put both hands on the seat of a recliner to get herself up. Tamsin had found her sitting there ten minutes later, waiting for her knee to settle. On Thursday, when the offer of a folding table came through, Tamsin had called her at home.

Ellis set down his coffee. “What’s coming in a van?”

“The folding table.”

“The chairs are coming at eleven.”

Tamsin looked at the intake book. The entry for the chairs had been crossed through in blue.

“I did that,” Ellis said. “They’re for Meena. I told her she could have all six.”

June put the bag of rags down.

“Where did you think they were going till she came?” Tamsin asked.

“In the back. She said she’d have her brother’s car.”

The chairs were beech, with rush seats. Ellis had gone to the donor’s flat the previous week to check whether the seats were sound. He had sat on every one. He told this part facing June, who asked if he’d bounced.

“Twice on each,” he said, and she laughed before she remembered to look at Tamsin.

Tamsin called Meena. She answered on the fourth ring, with a boy asking for toast in the background. Her brother’s car was at the garage. He had said he could borrow a car after two, so she’d been going to ring and ask how late they were open.

“One,” Tamsin said. “I can stay till quarter past.”

There was a pause during which Meena apparently put bread in the toaster.

“I’ve got two chairs now,” she said. “There’s five of us. I can take them on the bus if you let me do it one at a time.”

Tamsin pictured Meena at the bus stop with a chair, making the same journey six times. She took down Meena’s number again and said she’d call back.

By ten the shop was busy enough that the question of the rectangle had to wait. A father and daughter came for a wardrobe and found that it was two inches taller than the alcove they’d measured. Ellis took them over to a low chest they’d passed on their way in. One drawer stuck. He pulled it out, rubbed the runner with a candle stub from his coat pocket, and had the girl slide it in again.

“Try it with one finger,” he said.

Across the room, Len was making out the collection slip for a woman who was taking a cot. She asked him whether the man with the van would bring it upstairs.

“Ask Tamsin,” Len said. “She puts it on the slip while you’re here. We used to find out about the stairs when we got there.”

The woman lived on the third floor. Tamsin wrote it down and asked about the turn on the landing. The woman held her hands apart, then brought them closer. Tamsin fetched a screwdriver so they could take the cot ends off before loading.

At ten to eleven, Len found her counting the chairs already on the shop floor, as though one of them might have somewhere else to be.

“I could get three in my estate,” he said.

“Would you have time for two trips?”

He took his phone out and looked at it. His daughter was playing in a match at one. He could drop off the first three on his way there.

Ellis said he could take the others in his hatchback. Len looked out the window at it.

“You’ve still got that sink in there.”

“I can take the sink out.”

“Where are you going to put the sink?”

Ellis looked around the room. Tamsin watched him arrive at the yellow rectangle.

The chair donor pulled up at eleven in a borrowed pickup. She had to return it by twelve. Ellis went out to help with the straps, and Tamsin stood in the doorway long enough to see him run his thumb across a place where the rush had lifted. He pressed it back into the weave and picked up the chair by its frame.

Tamsin called the woman with the folding table. The woman answered through the van speakers. She was two streets away.

“I’ve got a space problem,” Tamsin said. “Could I call you when we have room for it?”

“I’m emptying my dad’s flat. The van goes back this afternoon.”

Tamsin could hear the indicator ticking.

“Do you want me to pull in anyway?” the woman asked.

Tamsin had said on Thursday that she could bring it straight in and someone would be there to help. She could see June flattening empty cartons at the counter, leaning her weight through the heel of one hand.

“I’m sorry,” Tamsin said. “I gave away the space twice. I can’t take it today.”

“All right,” the woman said. The ticking stopped. After a moment she said, “I could have left it with his neighbour this morning.” Then she said goodbye.

The six chairs came through the door in pairs. Ellis set them in the rectangle with all their backs facing the same way. When he came to the counter for the intake book, Tamsin told him about the call.

“I crossed the chairs out because they were spoken for,” he said.

“I read it as cancelled.”

He looked down at the page. “I can see how you would.”

He drew a small box beside the crossed-out entry and wrote *Meena* inside it. Then he put the pen down so Tamsin could add the collection time.

June had heard enough from the other side of the counter to know the table was gone. She folded one of the pillowcases over her arm.

“I can do another week with the bedding in bags,” she said. “People keep asking what sizes we’ve got, though.”

Tamsin looked at the counter. Half of it was taken up by a dead printer and a tray of screws sorted into yoghurt pots. She asked Len where the printer was going. He said it had been going to electrical recycling since Easter and carried it out to his car. June put the screws on the shelf beneath the till. Working side by side, she and Tamsin folded and labelled four sets before the next customer needed the counter to sign a slip. June put the unfinished set in a pillowcase and hung it from her wrist while he wrote.

Meena called just after twelve. Her brother had borrowed a people carrier. They arrived at ten to one with two booster seats in the back and a bag of groceries that Meena moved to the front footwell. Ellis and her brother took the chairs out. Meena stayed inside a moment, running her palm over the seat of the last one.

“My youngest will push his peas through this,” she said.

“Give me a ring if he gets a whole carrot through,” Ellis said.

After they left, June took her bag of rags and two pillowcases home to wash. Len had already gone to the match, with the printer in the front seat. Tamsin turned off the radio. Ellis brought the sign in from the bus stop and set it beside the door, where she would have to move it to sweep.

Outside, she locked up. Ellis stayed beside her while she pulled on the handle to check it.

“I can come in early next week,” he said. “There’s a drawer in that tallboy I want to look at.”

“I’ll be here at nine.”

For a moment she held the key between them while she dug in her bag for her own keys. Ellis reached out, then took the empty coffee cup she had tucked under her arm. He carried both cups to the bin at the bus stop. By the time he turned back, she had worked the shop key onto her ring, between her front door key and the little one for the meter cupboard. She put the ring in her coat pocket and went home.
