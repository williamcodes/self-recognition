The woman whose kitchen floor they had scratched came in at nine with a piece of blue linoleum in her hand. Tamsin had been in charge of Sundays for three weeks and had yet to see the form for this.

“I don’t want a new floor,” the woman said. “I want somebody to put the feet back on the dresser before it goes to anybody else.”

“It’s coming back?”

“It’s in my hallway. We couldn’t get it around the turn.”

Tamsin took down her number and arranged a collection for Tuesday. Then she found four felt pads, put them in an envelope, and stapled the envelope to the collection slip.

“There,” said Mo, who drove the exchange van. “That’s what I like about working for you. Tuesday actually finds out what happened on Sunday.”

At ten past nine Ellis came in with a bag of pears. He had run the Sunday shift for eleven years. Since giving it up he had been back every week, bringing things people hadn’t asked him for and were generally glad to have. He put the pears by the kettle and went to hang his coat on the hook where Tamsin’s coat now hung. After a moment he put the two coats on the same hook.

“Early lunch for me today,” he said. “I’ve got my granddaughter at half twelve.”

From the back room, Priya called, “Before you go, will you show me how you get that drawer out?”

Ellis went. A minute later there was a wooden clack, followed by Priya saying, “Oh, for heaven’s sake. I’ve been lifting the wrong end for a year.”

Tamsin opened the appointment book. At eleven, a folding table from the Methodist hall. Priya had spent two Sundays on a low stool sorting donated curtain rings and cabinet handles into tubs on her knees. Tamsin had measured the clear space in the back room and told her the table would fit there open, even with somebody working at it.

At eleven thirty, in green ink she hadn’t seen before, somebody had written *6 beech chairs. G. Morris. For Ida.*

“Ellis?”

He came out wiping old drawer wax on his trousers.

“Did those chairs just go in the book?”

“Thursday. I was passing. Graham’s clearing his dad’s place and Ida’s been after a set of six since Easter.”

“Is Ida collecting this morning?”

“I told her we’d keep them till her boy could borrow a van.”

The back room had once been a ticket office. You could fit six dining chairs in it, three against each long wall, or you could open a folding table in it. The front of the shop was full, down to a narrow passage that had to stay clear from the street door to the yard. The yard itself had no roof. Rain ticked against its bins.

“Where did you think they’d go?” Tamsin asked.

Ellis pointed to the back room. Through its open door Priya could be seen on her stool, separating a fistful of cup hooks from a length of yellow string.

“I’ve promised that space to Priya.”

“So I gather,” he said.

He picked a pear out of the bag, examined a bruise, and put it back.

At ten a man bought a bedside cabinet and carried it away under one arm. Its departure left a patch of carpet hardly bigger than a tea towel. Tamsin stood looking at the patch until Mo came up beside her.

“In case you’re wondering,” he said, “they won’t fit in the van today either. I’ve got the hospital beds to take out. Best part of my week, driving round with two empty beds behind me and everybody else on the road giving me plenty of room.”

“Do you have Ida’s number?”

“Ellis will. He’s the reason she comes here. First time she turned up she had the measurements for a wardrobe on the back of a gas bill. He made us measure the stairs as well. Saved me carrying a wardrobe up four flights to discover what a tape measure could’ve told us in the car park.”

Tamsin found Ellis in the office, already on the telephone.

“Yes, all six,” he was saying. “No, love, I know what I said.”

Tamsin held out her hand. Ellis hesitated, then passed her the receiver.

“Ida? It’s Tamsin at the exchange. We’re trying to work out where your chairs can go.”

“I told Ellis I could take them today if somebody brought them. My boy can get a van Wednesday, maybe. Do I have to get him out of bed to find out? He’s only just come off nights.”

“No. Leave him there. Are those the four flights of stairs by the baths?”

“Three. Mo counts the pavement.”

“Would you mind if the man who’s giving them away came straight to yours? I haven’t asked him yet.”

There was a pause. “I’d mind him having my address. I don’t know him.”

“All right,” Tamsin said. On the blotter in front of her she had written *Graham → Ida*. She crossed it out.

“I know it sounds silly.”

“You can say who comes to your house.”

When she put the phone down Ellis was reading the fire notice, which he had typed himself and could probably have recited from memory.

“She lets Mo in,” he said.

“Mo’s got beds.”

“I know he’s got beds.”

At twenty to eleven Priya emerged carrying three tubs. A red half-moon showed on each knee below the hem of her shorts.

“Can we settle something before the table comes?” she said. “Every time I say my knees hurt, somebody tells me their knees hurt too. I’m nineteen. Apparently I’m going to find this funny when I’m sixty.”

“I haven’t forgotten the table,” Tamsin said.

“Good. Because I had another idea. Could Graham have the hospital beds?”

For a second Tamsin pictured an elderly man sitting up in a narrow adjustable bed where his dining set had once stood.

“He’s got a furniture van,” Priya said. “That’s what it says in the book under his name. Could he do Mo’s deliveries, and Mo do the chairs?”

“He’s a donor,” said Ellis. “We don’t usually put them to work.”

“Didn’t you start here by bringing in a lamp?” Mo asked him.

Graham, when asked, said his insurance didn’t cover delivering hospital equipment. He also said he had to get his hired van back by noon or pay another forty pounds. At five to eleven, before anybody had thought of anything else, he arrived with the chairs.

Tamsin went outside to look. The seats were blue vinyl, each a slightly different blue. On the underside of each chair somebody had written a name in ballpoint.

“My brothers and sisters,” Graham said. “Dad used to tighten the screws and put them back by our places. God knows why. Same backsides, near enough.”

There was a photograph Tamsin could have taken of those names. There was a little card she could have written to go with the chairs. She was beginning to understand how Ellis could spend eleven years doing this.

“Have you eaten?” she asked.

“Why?”

“Because I’m hoping you’ll give me ten minutes.”

Back inside she found the folding table coming in sideways through the street door. The woman at the leading end was walking backwards. Tamsin took her elbow and guided her around the till. They opened the table in the back room. Priya set down her tubs and straightened up to her full height, which was considerable.

Mo stood watching. “Can I say something about these bloody beds? They’re going to the same place. The care home’s got a loading bay, a lift and two porters. All this talk as if I were taking them to Edinburgh. I can be back in half an hour.”

Tamsin looked at the wall clock. Eleven oh four. “Graham needs to leave before half past.”

“Well,” said Ellis, taking his coat off the hook, “somebody had better sit with Graham and find out exactly how long it takes to get to the hire place. And somebody else could get a move on.”

Mo was back at eleven thirty-one. Graham had left at eleven twenty-six. For five minutes, under the dry awning of the exchange, six people had each sat on a blue chair. Ellis had the one marked *Geraldine*. A customer who had only come in for a lampshade sat on *Paul*. The council required the pavement to be kept clear, so they sat in a tight line against the window with their knees turned sideways. Rain came off the awning and splashed their shoes. Priya, looking out through the glass from her new table, laughed so hard she had to sit down herself.

By twelve twenty Mo had Ida’s signature on his delivery sheet and a firm correction pencilled underneath the address. Three flights.

At one, Ellis’s daughter came for him with the little girl asleep in the car. Tamsin had finished the cash sheet. Priya was putting the lid on a tub labelled *Things we have exactly one of*.

Ellis separated the exchange key from his ring. It was the original, longer than the duplicate Tamsin had been issued, and you had to pull the door towards you to make it turn.

“Keep yours,” he said. “Mo ought to have this one. He’s often here first.”

Tamsin looked at Mo, who shook his head. “I’m here first because I like twenty minutes with the crossword.”

“Then I’ll take it home,” Tamsin said. “I’ll give the spare to the weekday office tomorrow.”

Ellis dropped the long key into her palm. “What time do you want me next Sunday?”

“Ten. Priya’s got a whole chest of drawers for you.”

His granddaughter woke as he got into the car. Through the window they saw her gravely accept the bruised pear.

Tamsin waited while Mo and Priya put on their coats. Outside, the six dry squares on the pavement had almost filled with rain. She pulled the door towards her and turned the long key, then put it on the ring that held her house key.
