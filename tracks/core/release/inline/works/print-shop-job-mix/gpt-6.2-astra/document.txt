**Reserve 60 hours for 20 recurring newsletters. Set initial acceptance limits for the rest of the quarter at 58 event posters, 42 menus, and 19 art prints.** At last quarter’s average production times, those 139 jobs would use 299.5 of the available 310 hours and earn $12,135 after materials. The remaining 10.5 hours give the two-person shop room to protect delivery dates. Posters earn the most per production hour, but 11 of 58 were late, so the shop should offer those slots only on dates it can meet.

A 12% materials increase leaves the acceptance order intact. It cuts the recommended mix’s contribution by $714.60, to $11,420.40. For one pricing experiment, test a 10% increase on comparable poster quotes. Posters provide the most opportunities to observe customer response, and the shop can measure whether a higher price improves earnings even if fewer customers accept.

I use **contribution** to mean revenue less materials spend. It is the amount available to cover staff pay, overhead, and profit. The supplied figures include the materials consumed by reprints and the production time spent on rework, so both costs of a difficult job already affect its measured productivity. The data give no wage or overhead rates from which to calculate net profit.

If a particular menu needs substantial layout work or another proof, use its estimated hours when deciding which order to accept.

This plan treats last quarter’s completed volumes as a guide to the demand the shop can fill at current prices. After reserving the newsletter hours, it allows for the previous volume of posters and menus, then books 19 of the previous 31 art-print jobs. The reductions from last quarter are four newsletters and 12 art prints. Together they release about 44.5 hours, covering the 34-hour capacity reduction and creating the scheduling reserve.

Holding 10.5 hours open is a judgment call. The quarterly totals show how many deliveries were late, but they do not show whether the cause was a crowded press schedule, slow customer approvals, or something else. The reserve amounts to roughly 3.4% of next quarter’s capacity. Because historical production times already include average rework, its purpose is to absorb variation around those averages and allow for work bunching up near a deadline.

Treat each limit as a ceiling across the quarter. When taking a poster order, check the hours available **before its promised delivery date** and enter the job against that week’s capacity. A vacant slot late in the quarter cannot help with an event taking place next Friday. Book recurring newsletters against their actual issue dates first so that both people see the same commitments when quoting turnaround times.

Release the reserve as the quarter progresses. If hours are tracking to plan, upcoming deadlines are covered, and art-print demand is still available, three more average art-print jobs would consume about 8.1 hours. That would bring bookings to 142 jobs, production to 307.6 hours, and expected contribution to $12,450, with about 2.4 hours left. This puts a dollar value on the initial decision to hold space open and gives the owner a way to earn it back once the delivery calendar is clearer.

## One pricing experiment

Test a **10% increase on comparable event-poster quotes**. The historical average was $100 in revenue and $30 in materials per poster job. For a job normally quoted at $100, test $110. At the same material use and production time, contribution rises from $70 to $80, and contribution per production hour rises from $44.13 to $50.43. Keep specifications and promised turnaround comparable so the shop can interpret the response to price.

Alternate eligible inquiries between the usual quote and the 10% higher quote. Record the quoted amount, whether the customer accepted, and the reason for any decline if offered. For accepted orders, record actual materials, hours, and delivery performance. Count only inquiries the shop had capacity to fulfill on the requested date. An order turned away because the schedule was full tells the owner little about what the customer would have paid.

Judge the test on **contribution per eligible inquiry**, alongside contribution per production hour. At historical costs, the higher quote breaks even per inquiry if its acceptance rate reaches 87.5% of the usual quote’s acceptance rate, since $70 divided by $80 equals 0.875. For example, if 80% accept the usual quote, 70% would need to accept the higher one. Keep the increase if the stronger contribution holds across comparable jobs and delivery performance is acceptable. A small difference based on only a handful of quotes warrants more observations.
