# Investment proposal for a shared pool of reusable produce crates

Approve a 12-week pilot with 150 reusable crates and a spending cap of $2,400, including the cost of any disposable cartons still needed. At the proposed usage and return rates, the pilot would replace 1,200 disposable cartons and cost $1,953, or $153 more than buying cartons alone for the same period. That premium buys a crate pool the cooperative can use in subsequent market periods. At the same volume and return rates, a second 12-week period would cost an estimated $753 before labor, transport, and ordinary wear. The board should decide whether to expand after it has measured those costs and closed out the final loans.

## Historical demand and pilot assumptions

| Comparable market month | Disposable cartons used | Sample crates borrowed | Returned within seven days | Seven-day return rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 420 | 100 | 92 | 92.0% |
| 2 | 510 | 120 | 108 | 90.0% |
| 3 | 600 | 140 | 126 | 90.0% |
| 4 | 470 | 110 | 99 | 90.0% |
| **Total** | **2,000** | **470** | **425** | **90.4%** |

The cooperative spent $2,400 on the 2,000 cartons used in the four historical months, an average of $600 per month. For planning, treat the pilot as three four-week market periods at the historical average of 500 cartons each. That gives a 12-week baseline of **1,500 cartons at $1.20 each, or $1,800**. The baseline covers recorded carton demand. The historical figures do not say whether sample crates carried additional volume or displaced cartons, so the sample loans inform the return assumption. During the pilot, count a crate loan as a carton replacement only when the grower confirms that it served a shipment that would otherwise have used one carton.

The base case assumes 1,200 such loans over 12 weeks, averaging 100 per week and covering 80% of projected carton demand. Assume 90% of loans come back within seven days, another 9% come back by day 14, and 1% are ultimately lost and charged to the replacement budget. The sample recorded only seven-day returns, so the 9% late recovery and 1% loss rates are planning assumptions. A returned crate must be washed and checked before it can be lent again. Each return is budgeted at $0.25, including returns washed after week 12.

Buying 150 crates leaves 30 storage positions free within the 180-crate limit. At 100 weekly loans, the base case implies roughly 10 crates from the prior week are still out at the next dispatch, with a smaller number still outstanding from earlier weeks. The extra crates give the cooperative room for late returns, washing delays, and uneven bookings. The target represents eight loans per initially purchased crate across the pool over 12 weeks. Individual crates will see different use, so the coordinator should track completed cycles by crate ID.

## Cost comparison

| 12-week cost | Cartons only | Base pilot | Lower-use, higher-loss pilot |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Initial crate purchase | $0 | $1,200 | $1,200 |
| Disposable cartons | $1,800 | $360 | $648 |
| Washing | $0 | $297 | $228 |
| Replacement crates | $0 | $96 | $384 |
| **Total** | **$1,800** | **$1,953** | **$2,460** |
| Difference from cartons only | — | +$153 | +$660 |

The base pilot buys 150 crates at $8, uses 300 cartons, washes 1,188 returned crates, and replaces 12 losses. It leaves **$447** under the spending cap for additional costs or demand variation. The $753 estimate for a subsequent period includes cartons, washing, and loss replacements. It implies a $1,047 margin against carton-only spending at the same volume. Paid coordination, additional transport, and replacement for ordinary wear would draw down that margin. For a crate that stays in circulation, each completed loan saves $0.95 after washing, so it takes nine completed loans to recover its $8 purchase price before those other costs.

The lower-use, higher-loss case assumes 960 loans, or 80 a week, with 85% returned within seven days, another 10% by day 14, and 5% ultimately lost. It uses 540 cartons and washes 912 returned crates. Replacing all 48 losses would exceed the spending cap by $60 even before other expenses. Deferring eight replacements would bring those modeled costs to $2,396 and leave 142 serviceable crates, assuming no other retirements. That leaves only $4 for other cash costs, so the treasurer should defer more purchases if needed. Each week, the treasurer should forecast remaining carton demand and washing costs before approving discretionary replacements. The coordinator should check that the available pool can cover confirmed bookings before any replacement is deferred.

## Operating rules and responsibilities

- **Bookings and custody.** The board appoints a pool coordinator to manage weekly bookings, dispatch, returns, and inventory. Growers provide expected quantities before market day. The coordinator allocates available clean crates against confirmed orders and scales allocations proportionally when demand exceeds supply. At pickup, the coordinator records the crate ID, grower, checkout date, and due date. The grower who checks out the crate is responsible for getting it back, including when a buyer or driver handles it.
- **Returns and losses.** Growers return crates within seven calendar days at the designated collection point. The coordinator sends a reminder at the due date, follows up on day 14, and reviews unresolved loans on day 21. The pilot budget assumes the cooperative pays for losses. After reviewing repeated overdue loans with a grower, the coordinator may limit further checkouts until the grower has a workable return arrangement. If a crate returns after being recorded as lost, the coordinator corrects the inventory and loss records.
- **Washing and storage.** The wash lead logs each returned crate, keeps dirty and clean stock apart, and checks washed crates for cracks, broken handles, or other damage before releasing them. Damaged crates stay out of circulation pending review. The coordinator counts clean, dirty, and quarantined crates against storage capacity when scheduling deliveries and replacement purchases.
- **Costs and participation.** Growers confirm the number of cartons displaced and report any packing, handling, or buyer issues. The treasurer reconciles purchases, wash charges, and replacements with the loan register and records paid and volunteer hours and any additional trips. The board reviews a short report at weeks 4 and 8 covering loans, verified carton displacement, returns, available clean stock, costs, and forecast spending through closeout. If circulation runs below target, the coordinator checks whether bookings, return logistics, or wash capacity are limiting use before proposing more purchases.

## Expansion decision

At week 12, review demand, service quality, and spending. Make the final decision after the last loans reach their day-21 review so they have the same opportunity to return as early loans. Use all loans whose seven-day deadline has passed to calculate the on-time return rate. Calculate ultimate recovery from loans that have completed the full follow-up window, and reconcile the resulting loss count with physical inventory and crate purchases.

Expand if at least 80% of actual eligible packing demand was served by crates, at least 90% of loans came back within seven days, and at least 99% were ultimately recovered. At the forecast demand of 1,500 carton-equivalent shipments, the usage threshold is 1,200 verified replacements. The board should also require spending within $2,400 and a projected cost below cartons alone at the next period’s expected volume, after valuing staff and volunteer time, transport, and ordinary wear. Check that buyers accepted the crates and that the wash lead can handle peak return volumes before authorizing more stock.

If the pilot meets those conditions, size the next purchase using confirmed peak bookings, observed turnaround time, and available storage. The existing site can hold up to 30 more crates if all 150 pilot crates are still in service and the full 180 positions are usable. Any larger purchase requires additional storage. If a threshold is missed but the records point to a fix, such as a better return location or a different wash schedule, keep the existing pool and run a limited retest with a defined target and budget. If demand or measured costs fail to support continued operation, end new lending, recover outstanding crates, and decide how to use the remaining stock.
