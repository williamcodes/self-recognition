# Annual pass expansion proposal

The cinema should expand the annual pass next season through a limited release, with further sales contingent on screening-level capacity and the financial performance of each group of buyers. The pilot shows that pass holders used the cinema regularly and that recorded admissions filled about 60% of available seats across four months. Some screenings still sold out, and the cinema lacks the showtime records needed to know where additional pass use would fit. A limited release gives the board a way to test whether the pass fills seats that would otherwise be empty, attracts paying guests, and earns enough to cover royalties and any ticket sales it replaces.

## What the pilot shows

| Month | Active passes | Pass-holder visits | Single tickets sold | Recorded admissions | Available seats | Seats filled | Visits per active pass |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 80 | 180 | 940 | 1,120 | 2,000 | 56.0% | 2.25 |
| February | 95 | 250 | 880 | 1,130 | 1,840 | 61.4% | 2.63 |
| March | 105 | 205 | 1,020 | 1,225 | 2,100 | 58.3% | 1.95 |
| April | 112 | 280 | 865 | 1,145 | 1,750 | 65.4% | 2.50 |
| **Total** | **392 pass-months** | **915** | **3,705** | **4,620** | **7,690** | **60.1%** | **2.33** |

The table treats each single ticket sold as one admission. The active-pass total counts a pass once for each month it appears in the reported figures. It gives a denominator for average monthly use, while the number of distinct pass holders remains unknown.

Active passes rose from 80 to 112, a 40% increase. Pass-holder visits accounted for about one in five recorded admissions across the pilot and nearly one in four in April. Average use ranged from 1.95 to 2.63 visits per active pass per month. The monthly figures show that pass holders made substantial use of the benefit. They do not show whether that use was spread evenly across holders or concentrated among a small number of frequent visitors. That distinction matters because the cinema earns the same 100 units on each pass while paying royalties on every visit.

The cinema recorded 3,070 unfilled seats across the pilot, including 605 in April, the fullest month by percentage. April had fewer admissions than March and a higher fill rate because it offered 350 fewer seats. Monthly fill rates therefore reflect changes in the number of seats offered as well as changes in attendance. For the expansion decision, the relevant question is whether spare seats are available at screenings pass holders would choose to attend.

## What remains uncertain

Single-ticket sales fell from 940 in January to 865 in April, while pass-holder visits rose from 180 to 280. One possible explanation is that some people bought passes and used them for films they would otherwise have paid to see. Other explanations are also consistent with the figures. Programming changed each month, available seats varied, and the distribution of screenings across nights may have affected attendance. March had the highest single-ticket sales of the four months even as the number of active passes rose. The pilot has no comparison that isolates the effect of passes from those other changes. The 75-ticket decline between January and April cannot serve as an estimate of sales lost to the pass.

Pass use can affect ticket sales in two ways. A pass holder can replace a ticket they would personally have bought, even at a screening with empty seats. A pass reservation can also take a seat someone else would have bought at a popular screening. The cinema knows some screenings sold out, but it did not record their times. The board cannot tell which nights or programs were constrained, how many seats pass holders used at those screenings, or whether anyone tried to buy a ticket after seats were gone. A sold-out screening establishes that all available seats were taken. Evidence of turnaways or waitlist demand would help estimate how many additional tickets could have sold.

The volunteer survey suggests another possibility. Pass holders may create ticket sales by inviting friends. The survey does not establish how many friends bought tickets, how often that happened, or whether those friends would have attended anyway. The finding is worth testing because just a few genuinely additional paid visits per pass could materially change the economics. Counting all companions as sales generated by the pass would overstate that effect.

Four months also leave much of the annual commitment unobserved. Early use could reflect enthusiasm after purchase, and later use could shift with the calendar or the program. The active-pass counts give no reliable measure of renewals or of how long each holder had been using the pass. The cinema can evaluate the pilot’s observed usage and royalty costs now. A full-year assessment requires following buyers from purchase through expiration.

## Financial implications

Each single ticket brings in 9 units and incurs a 2-unit screening royalty, leaving 7 units before other costs. The pilot’s 3,705 single tickets thus generated 33,345 units in gross sales and 25,935 after their screening royalties, assuming each ticket incurred one royalty. The 915 pass-holder visits incurred another 1,830 units in royalties. Pass revenue for the period cannot be reconstructed from the active-pass counts. The cinema needs actual purchase and renewal records, including dates and amounts, to determine how much it collected and which annual obligations those sales created.

At the individual pass level, the 100-unit price covers 50 visits’ worth of screening royalties. Dividing the observed 915 visits by 392 active pass-months gives 2.33 visits per pass-month. If that rate held over an entire year, a pass would average about 28 visits, incur 56 units in royalties, and leave 44 units before any effect on single-ticket sales and before other costs. This is a planning scenario. It assumes the four-month usage rate continues across the year, including months with different films and schedules.

At 28 visits, each single-ticket sale lost reduces that 44-unit contribution by another 7 units. Six lost sales leave 2 units, and seven produce a 5-unit loss, before other costs. Each additional paid visit attributable to the pass adds 7 units on the same basis. For example, a pass holder who makes 28 visits, replaces eight paid admissions, and generates three additional paid admissions would contribute 9 units before other costs. The calculation counts the 2-unit royalty on every pass visit and uses the 7-unit ticket margin to value any change in paid admissions.

The board should therefore judge the pass against expected results under the existing season plan. An annual pass can cover its holder’s royalties while still reducing the cinema’s contribution if it replaces enough paid visits. Conversely, a pass that fills spare seats and brings paying guests can contribute financially even if the holder visits often. If extra bookings create measurable staffing, booking, or other operating costs, those costs belong in the expansion decision as well.

## Proposal for next season

Keep the annual price at 100 units and authorize an initial tranche of up to 20 additional passes above the April level of 112 active passes. Use April as a reference point for the size of the test. Twenty is a proposed limit on the initial commitment, chosen because the cinema lacks showtime data for a capacity-based estimate. At the observed average rate, those passes would add around 47 visits in a typical month. The booking records should show where those visits land. Before selling the tranche, the cinema should also define the terms that will apply for the full annual pass period and budget for the royalties through expiration.

Start collecting screening-level data when next season’s booking opens. Each screening record should include its date and time, program, seats available for sale, paid bookings, pass bookings, attendance, and whether and when it sold out. Record waitlist requests or turnaways when possible, along with canceled reservations and no-shows. Record pass purchases, renewals, and expirations separately, and use a consistent pass identifier to follow visit frequency over time. Where the booking system can link paid companion tickets to pass reservations, use that link to count companion attendance. A short follow-up question asking whether the guest would have come independently can inform a range of estimates for additional ticket sales.

Review the results after the first two months of the expanded season. Compare screenings with similar programs and time slots where possible, and examine busy screenings individually. For the financial review, project annual usage under several assumptions, including heavier use than the pilot average. Show the contribution under a range of estimates for ticket sales lost and gained. Give the board a view based on zero additional companion sales as well as a view that credits the portion supported by booking and survey evidence. Separately report whether recent sellouts produced waitlists or turnaways and how much pass use occurred at those screenings.

Release another tranche only if the contribution remains positive under a conservative set of assumptions and the screening records show room for additional use. If the first tranche leaves the board unsure how much paid demand is being displaced, maintain that sales limit while collecting more data. If pressure is concentrated on particular nights or programs, use that evidence to evaluate whether a future pass with explicit off-peak terms would fit the cinema’s schedule. Honor the terms of passes already sold.

The board should also track the community benefit it expects from the pass. If one goal is to help residents attend more regularly, report how many distinct holders use it, how usage is distributed, and how often holders report attending films they would otherwise have skipped. The board can then decide explicitly whether any financial shortfall is worth funding for that benefit. The decision at the end of the season should combine screening-level capacity, paid-ticket effects, and observed use over the annual term before the cinema commits to a larger rollout.
