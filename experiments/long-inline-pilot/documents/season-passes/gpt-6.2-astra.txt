# Annual pass proposal for the next season

The board should retain the 100-unit annual pass and authorize a staged expansion. The pilot shows that pass holders used the cinema regularly and that the cinema had spare seats in every month. It also records sold-out screenings. Both facts matter. An extra pass holder who attends a quiet screening can bring in subscription income at little additional cost. An extra pass holder who would otherwise buy single tickets, or who takes the last seat a single-ticket customer wanted, can reduce the money available for programming. The pilot establishes how often members attended in aggregate. It leaves open which of those circumstances was most common.

For next season, the cinema should offer renewals and release an initial batch of up to 20 additional passes. That batch would generate up to 2,000 units in gross receipts and expand the active base by about 18% relative to April if all 112 passes remain active. Twenty is a proposed trial size the board can review after one quarter. The evidence does not establish an optimal membership ceiling. The board should reserve a second batch of up to 20 for a separate decision, informed by screening-level demand, the purchasing history of new members where available, and an estimate of the cost of honoring their passes for a full year.

Active passes rose from 80 to 112, a 40% increase. Pass holders accounted for nearly one visit in five across the pilot. The average of 2.33 visits per active pass per month uses 392 active pass-months, the sum of the four monthly counts. A person with an active pass throughout contributes four pass-months. These figures tell the board how much attendance the membership base generated, but they cannot distinguish broad participation from a pattern in which a few frequent visitors accounted for much of the use. The increase of 32 active passes is also a net change. Without issue and expiry dates, the cinema cannot reconstruct gross sales, renewals, or cash receipts from those counts.

April illustrates why the choice of measure matters. It had the highest occupancy and the most pass visits, yet March drew 80 more visits overall. April offered 350 fewer seats. February and April combined relatively high pass use with lower single-ticket sales, which could suggest that some people switched from tickets to passes. Monthly changes in films, screening schedules, and seat supply could produce the same pattern. Four months with different programs provide no reliable estimate of how many single tickets the cinema would have sold without the pass.

## What the seats and receipts can tell the board

Across the pilot, 3,070 seats went unused. That makes it plausible that the cinema could welcome more members at some performances. It gives little guidance about a particular Friday evening. A vacant seat on Tuesday cannot accommodate someone turned away on Friday, and moving a member to Tuesday only helps if that person wants to see the film then. Because the sold-out screening times were not recorded, the board cannot tell whether scarcity was concentrated in one popular title, recurred on the same night each week, or affected screenings with substantial pass use. Even a sellout with many members present would not by itself establish lost ticket sales. That requires evidence that someone else wanted a seat.

The financial records support a partial calculation. Single tickets generated 33,345 units. The 4,620 visits incurred 9,240 units in royalties, including 1,830 for pass-holder visits. This leaves 24,105 units from single-ticket receipts after the royalties on **all** visits, before pass income and other operating costs. It is a useful subtotal for reconciling the pilot accounts. Adding 100 units for every active pass each month would count the same annual entitlement repeatedly. Even a complete record of pass sales during the pilot would need to be read alongside the remaining months of access the cinema owes each buyer.

The economics of one annual pass show which missing evidence matters most. Suppose a new member makes 28 visits over the year, roughly the result of extending the pilot’s average monthly use across 12 months. That is a planning scenario, since summer programming, seasonal attendance, and the habits of new buyers may differ. The pass brings in 100 units and those visits incur 56 in royalties. If every visit is additional and there is room for everyone who wants to attend, 44 units remain before other costs. Under those assumptions, 50 visits would use the entire pass price in royalties.

Now suppose the same member would have bought seven single tickets without the pass. Those tickets would have contributed 49 units after royalties. The pass still contributes 44 units on its own, but the cinema ends up **5 units worse off** than it would have been selling the seven tickets. If a different customer also loses a seat they would have bought, the cinema forgoes a further 7 units of ticket contribution, assuming that customer never rebooks. This distinction matters when evaluating crowded shows. A customer who switches to another performance and buys a ticket there presents a service concern, while a customer who abandons the purchase also represents lost income.

The volunteer survey suggests a possible benefit on the other side of the calculation. If a member brings a friend who buys a 9-unit ticket, would otherwise have stayed home, and uses an available seat, the cinema gains 7 units after the royalty. That one visit would cover the 5-unit shortfall in the seven-ticket example. The survey gives the board a reason to investigate this effect. It supplies neither the number of paying guests nor evidence about what they would otherwise have done. Counting every companion as new business would overstate the case for expansion, particularly if friends already attended together before one bought a pass.

## How to run the expansion

Keep the price and existing booking terms for the first batch. Changing the price, membership limit, and access rules together would leave the board guessing which change affected demand. Offer the places through the cinema’s usual public channels and record applications beyond the cap on a waiting list. Track renewals separately. If many existing members leave, selling 20 new passes may largely replace that loss. The board should see the number of active passes alongside gross sales and the size of the waiting list.

Give the box office one screening record it can complete as part of closing each performance. Record the title, date, start time, seats offered for sale, single tickets sold, pass reservations, actual admissions by ticket type, and whether and when booking closed because the show was full. For sold-out shows, record waiting-list requests or observed turnaways and, where practical, whether those customers obtained a seat at a later performance. A reservation count alone can make a show appear full even when no-shows leave seats empty. Recording both bookings and admissions would help the cinema decide whether a reservation-release rule could recover seats before it considers limiting member access.

At enrollment, ask new members how many single tickets they bought in the previous year and whether they expect to visit more often with the pass. Where members agree and records exist, use their actual prior purchases to check the estimates. Track visits by pass and issue date so a person who joined late in the quarter is compared with others who had similar time to attend. A short, optional guest question at selected performances can establish whether a paying visitor came with a member and whether they expect they would have attended anyway. Report the number of responses as well as the answers. A handful of enthusiastic respondents should carry correspondingly little weight in the financial forecast.

The programming team can use these records before the quarter ends. If one title repeatedly sells out while another performance of the same film has space, tell members and other customers about the available showing when they book. If demand exceeds capacity across all showings of a title, an extra performance may be worth considering against its staffing and operating costs. Compare similar titles and time slots when judging the result. A quieter audience after a blockbuster leaves the schedule says little about whether an intervention worked.

## The decision after one quarter

Before releasing the second batch, the board should review a full-year contribution forecast for the first 20 new passes, with higher-use and lower-use cases. Start with actual pass receipts, deduct projected royalties on member visits, deduct the contribution from single tickets members would likely have purchased, and allow for other paying customers who lost sales at full performances. Add the contribution from plausibly additional paying guests and deduct any extra operating costs. Show a case with no guest benefit so the board can see whether the financial decision depends on an effect the survey has yet to establish. Keep assumptions visible, especially where one more former ticket purchase per member would change the decision.

Release the second batch if the forecast remains positive under a reasonable cautious case and screening records show that the cinema can accommodate the added demand without recurring turnaways at popular performances. Hold at the first batch if the financial result depends on optimistic guest assumptions or if the same time slots repeatedly leave customers without seats. Where the quarter contains too few comparable screenings to judge, keep collecting records through the next programming cycle. Honor the annual terms already sold throughout that review.

The board may also decide that affordable repeat attendance merits a modest subsidy. In that case, it should name the amount it is prepared to spend and assess whether the pass reaches the people it hopes to serve. That gives the cinema two defensible grounds for expansion: a pass that helps pay for the program, or an explicitly funded access benefit. The staged proposal lets the board pursue either purpose while learning where seats are available, who gains access, and what the annual commitment costs.
