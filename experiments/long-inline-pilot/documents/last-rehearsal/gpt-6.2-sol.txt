The first time Ruth said, “You can let yourself out,” nobody moved. Ellis had already left the microphone. He stood at the end of the carpet with his script rolled against his thigh. At the effects table, Nina lifted the brass latch and tried it again.

There was a faint tick.

“That’s it?” Ruth asked.

“It goes down,” Nina said. “It just doesn’t come back.”

She held up the little board on which the latch was mounted. A spring had broken inside the casing. She had found half of it in the bottom of the box, under two coconuts and a bag of aquarium gravel.

From the control room, Len said, “Take it from ‘You can.’”

“You can let yourself out,” Ruth said.

Nina pushed the latch up with her thumb, then let it fall. A tick and a clack, with a small interval between.

“Good enough,” Len said.

“Other way round,” Ruth said.

Nina looked at her.

“There’s a clack when you lift it and a tick when it catches. That one sounds like somebody locking a suitcase.”

Nina set the board down gently, which made a third sound, a hollow wooden knock. “How many people do you think could tell you what that latch sounds like?”

“None of them.”

“All right.”

“They’ll still know.”

Through the glass Len showed them five fingers. It was six minutes past seven. At half past, the station went live to the school board meeting, and the studio had to be empty. Tonight they were rehearsing in the clothes they’d come in, parkas over chair backs, a grocery bag under Ruth’s music stand with a tub of yogurt going soft inside it. The following night was the broadcast. Nina, who normally read the school lunch menus and recorded the notices for the animal shelter, was filling in for Bert. He’d had the cataract in his right eye done on Monday. He had left her a page of instructions, including a sketch of where to put the microphone for the grapefruit crossing the kitchen floor. About the latch he had written DOOR.

The serial had been on Thursday nights for eleven years. Ruth played Iona, who let rooms over a hardware store. Learners of English called the station sometimes to ask what Iona had said. People who had grown up in town called to correct the prices. A man who plowed the county roads once sent in a drawing of the building, showing the stairs on the outside, and Ruth had turned it over on the table and drawn the stairs on the inside. She had never asked Len which drawing he kept.

Nina turned to the script. “Could we have him go on ‘out’? I can get his steps clean if he starts there.”

“He waits for the latch.”

“Why?”

“He’s waiting for her to open it for him.”

Nina looked from Ruth to Ellis. He had unrolled his script. He was twenty-three and played a man of thirty-seven. When he needed to be tired, he bent his knees.

“I thought I opened it myself,” he said.

“You do, eventually.”

“Have we been doing that the whole time?”

“Every time you leave angry.”

Len’s talkback clicked. “Let’s hear the eventually.”

Ellis returned to the microphone. In the scene he had come to get a reference from Iona. He had lived upstairs for eight years, and now he wanted a place with a yard, even though he had no children and was allergic to grass. Iona had written that he always paid in cash. The prospective landlord wanted to know if he was dependable.

“You could say I was quiet,” Ellis read.

“I could say you had good curtains.”

“They were there when I moved in.”

“Yes,” Ruth said. The word was meant to sound as though Iona had enjoyed something without knowing it until now. That had come easily in Ruth’s kitchen that morning. In the studio, she could hear herself trying to be pleased.

Ellis folded the reference. The paper by his microphone was a sheet of brown wrapping cut down to letter size. Ordinary paper had vanished in the mix when they first tried it.

“You can let yourself out,” Ruth said.

She heard him draw in breath for a line he didn’t have. It was an excellent breath. There was room in it for a person to reconsider. Then there was room for the latch, and after that there was just room.

“Walking,” Len said through the speaker.

Ellis started across the carpet. Nina took two steps in Bert’s box of linoleum, missed the third because she was watching Ellis, and produced the fourth after he’d stopped.

Ruth laughed. She couldn’t help it. It sounded as if he’d left the building and come back for his hat.

Nina took her foot out of the box. “I could do it if everybody stood still.”

Ellis went back to his mark.

“I mean you, too,” Nina said to Ruth. “Every time he goes, you turn your page.”

Ruth looked down. Her thumb was under the corner. The next scene took place three weeks later and began with Iona peeling an orange. The script said nothing about what she did after the man left. Ruth always turned there, taking care to lift the sheet by the edge so it wouldn’t drag across the stand.

Len came in for the latch. He shook it beside his ear. “Bert might have another one.”

“He said there’s only that one,” Nina said.

“When did you talk to Bert?”

“Last night. He asked me to read him the script. He wanted to know if the man took the reference.”

“Does he?” Ellis asked.

Ruth pointed to the sheet of wrapping paper in his hand.

“I could leave it,” he said.

“Then what are you going to show the landlord?”

“I thought he’d already seen it.”

There had been seven episodes about the reference. In one, Iona had gone downstairs to the hardware store to borrow a pen. In another, the man had brought up a bowl of plums to show that he was in no particular hurry. Ruth could remember who had played the shop assistant when they’d gone down for the pen, and the noise of the stone Bert had sucked clean for the last plum. She couldn’t remember whether the landlord had seen the letter. Len took off his glasses. They had four minutes.

Nina went to the studio door and pressed down the handle. It let out a nylon squeak. Beyond it, two folding chairs waited by the drinking fountain for members of the public to come in and speak at the school board meeting. The board members were meeting across town. Anybody who didn’t have a phone could make a comment from the studio.

“No,” Ruth said.

“I’m getting my bag.”

Nina brought in a canvas bag with a broken zipper, reached into it, and drew out a spoon. She tried the bowl of it against the iron microphone base. Too high. She laid the spoon flat against the latch board and slid it off the edge.

Ruth raised her head.

“Do it again,” she said.

Nina did. There was a scrape followed by a little jump in the wood as the bowl passed the edge. It was close enough that Ruth felt her own wrist turn. Nina took a nail from the effects box and tapped it once against the back of the spoon. That supplied the catch.

“Now if I could do that with my feet,” she said.

Len went back to the booth. Ruth took the spoon. It was warm from Nina’s hand. Nina showed her where to start, with the spoon almost off the board, and how lightly the nail had to touch. They moved the effects microphone nearer the music stand, and Len asked Ruth for a line to check the spill.

“You can let yourself out,” she said.

Something in the speaker popped.

“Again. Look where you were looking before.”

She had been looking at Ellis. She looked at him now and found him studying the reference, though there was nothing on it but the blue smudge that told them which side was quieter. He could see the smudge from where he held the paper. He turned it over as if he expected something on the other side.

“You can let yourself out,” Ruth said.

She lowered the spoon to the wood. Ellis didn’t move. When Bert did the latch he watched the script and took the gap every time. Ruth had thought of that gap as hers. Bert would be sitting at home tomorrow with his head tilted away from the radio to keep the sound on his good side. He would know how long she took, and whether she had meant to take that long.

Ellis lifted his eyes. He seemed about to fold the paper again, smaller this time. For a moment Ruth wanted him to put it down. She could have said something about the cash, about the trouble she’d gone to finding a pen. One of the plums had been split, and Iona had eaten that one first. Ruth moved her thumb. The spoon slid off the edge of the board.

At the far end of the studio, Nina took the first step. She took the second a little faster. Ruth tapped the nail, and Nina stopped with the sole of one shoe still making contact with the linoleum. She eased her weight onto it until the floor gave a tiny complaint. Then nothing. In the glass Ruth could see Len watching the second hand pass the top of the clock.

“Keep that,” he said, and dropped the talkback.

Ellis was still holding the reference. The board meeting music came up faintly through the wall. Out in the hall a woman took one of the folding chairs, keeping her coat on, and arranged a stack of papers in her lap. Nina crossed to the studio door to let her in. She had to lift the handle first and then pull it down, a thing everyone who worked at the station did by feel. Ruth heard the squeak she’d heard a few minutes earlier. Then the woman’s papers slid off her lap and fanned out on the floor.

Ellis laid the reference on the nearest chair and stooped to help. Nina held the door open with her heel. The woman gathered the pages as he handed them to her, turning each one right side up and checking the number at the bottom. Ruth stayed by the effects table. She put the nail back in the box and lifted the spoon to carry it to Nina. The bowl had left a clean stripe in the dust on the board. She set it down again, a little farther back, where tomorrow she could find the place with her thumb.
