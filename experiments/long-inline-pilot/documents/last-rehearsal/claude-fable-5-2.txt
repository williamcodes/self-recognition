# The Latch

The door was eleven inches wide and stood on the effects table between the gravel tray and the half coconut nobody had used since 1998. It had a brass knob, a frame of unpainted pine, and a thumb latch that for twenty-six years had made the sound of Dr. Aurelia Finch's surgery door closing on a patient. Now it made a sound like a spoon dropped in a drawer.

Petra worked it again. Clack. Something behind the plate had sheared, and the tongue no longer rode up the strike and fell. It just hit.

"That's not it," said Ines Varga, from the far side of the cast microphone, without lowering her script.

"No."

"Do it once more."

Petra did it once more. In the control room window Dov's head came up from the board. The talkback clicked. "What am I hearing?"

"The latch is gone," Petra said.

"Gone how?"

"Broken. Inside. I can open it up at the break."

"We have forty minutes of studio before the choir needs it for their thing. Keep going. Ines, from 'You'll want to keep that elevated.'"

They kept going. The show was *Marrow Road*, Thursday nights at seven-thirty on KVLT, a station whose signal covered two valleys and part of a third when the weather was right. It ran live from Studio B, which had been the Grange hall's cloakroom. Petra was a relief board operator who ran the overnight on weekends and had been asked nine days ago to do effects because Sol Brannigan had gone off a ladder cleaning his gutters and broken his wrist in two places. Sol had made the door. Sol had made most of the table. He had left her a cue script marked in three colors of pencil and a note that said *You'll be fine, don't let them rush the kettle.*

She was fine with the kettle. She was fine with footsteps on gravel, on boards, on the square of linoleum; she had learned to walk two shoes with her hands so that they sounded like a man with somewhere to be. She had the telephone bell and the cash drawer and the wet chamois in a bowl that was, in tonight's episode, a calf being born. She came in on the cue lights and got out clean.

"You'll want to keep that elevated," Ines said, in Dr. Finch's voice, which was lower than her own and unhurried. "And I'll want to see you Monday. Not Tuesday, Oskar. Monday."

"Monday," said Winston, who played Oskar Thule and four other men.

Footsteps, boards, going away. Petra walked the shoes off. Then the door. Clack.

Ines stopped. She didn't say anything this time. She only stopped, the way a person stops on a stair that isn't there, and the next line, which was hers, alone, to the empty surgery, went unsaid for three full seconds. Dov let the silence stand, then clicked in.

"Ines."

"I know. I'm sorry." She took off her glasses. She was sixty-eight and had been Dr. Finch since she was forty-two, since before the station had a website, since the show was recorded to tape in the mornings. "I can't play off that."

"It's a rehearsal."

"It's Thursday tomorrow."

"Petra will fix it at the break."

Petra said, "I'll look at it at the break."

Ines turned her head, and Petra understood the difference between those two sentences had been heard.

At the break she took the door into the corridor where the light was better and backed out the four screws with the driver on her knife. The spring was in two pieces. It was a flat leaf spring, bent to a shape nobody sold, blued and thin as a fingernail, and it had cracked across the bend, where it had flexed every Thursday since the first Bush administration. Sol had not bought this latch. He had taken it off something. There were older screw holes in the plate that didn't match the pine.

Ines came out with her tea. She looked at the parts laid on the windowsill.

"His mother's pantry," she said. "The house on Teller Street. He took it off the door the week they sold."

"I can't fix a spring like that. Not by tomorrow. Maybe not ever. I could bend something close out of a hacksaw blade but it'd take a few tries to get the temper."

"So what do we do?"

"There are two other latches in Sol's cabinet. One's a rim latch, heavier. And I can pull a door from the library, we've got about thirty."

"A recording."

"For one night."

"Of some other door."

Petra put the knife away. She had thought about how to say the next part, and had decided to say it flatly. "Nobody at home is going to know. It's a door closing. It's under a second long and it goes out through a compressor and a transmitter and comes out of a kitchen radio next to a running tap. I listened to the air checks. On air it's a thump with a little tick on the front."

"A tick and then a thump."

"On a good radio."

"That's what it is, though. The tick is the tongue going up. The thump is it coming down." Ines set her tea on the sill, beside the spring. "You think I'm being precious."

"I think you've heard it from four feet away for twenty-six years and they've heard it from across a room."

"Every week, though. Some of them every week since the start." She wasn't arguing hard; she seemed to be working it out as she went. "I get letters. Fewer now. A woman in Pruett wrote to say she shells peas to us. A man used to write who drove a milk tanker, he'd time his route so he was on the flat stretch by the reservoir where we come in clear. Do you know how every one of those scenes ends, the surgery scenes? Somebody leaves, and the door goes, and then I say something to nobody. That's the part people quote back to me. The line after the door. They never mention the door."

"Then they won't mention it tomorrow."

"No," Ines said. "They won't know what to mention. They'll just think I was a bit off."

Winston put his head around the corner. "Dov says two minutes, and he says he doesn't care if it's a mousetrap as long as it's on the light."

Petra screwed the plate back on over nothing, so it wouldn't rattle, and went to the cabinet.

They ran the scene with the rim latch. It was a good sound, to be fair to it; it had weight, and a little ring after. Ines said her line to the empty surgery and it came out as a line said to a studio. She shook her head before Dov could speak.

"It's a different house," she said. "That's a front door. That's a door with weather on the other side of it."

They ran it with the library. Dov fired it from the control room, a clean interior door, and it came down the monitor into the studio perfectly, and Ines missed it altogether, because there was nothing on the table for her to hear with the side of her face. She came in late and flat.

"I can't see you do it," she said to Petra, apologetically. "I never look. But I can't see you do it."

Dov clicked in. "Twenty-two minutes, friends."

Petra stood with her hands on the table. Her view had not changed: no pea-sheller in Pruett would tell one door from another through a five-inch speaker. But she had been watching the cue light and the script for nine days and now she'd been made to watch the actor. The door was a cue. Petra had been thinking of it as a thing sent out to the audience, and it was that, a little. Mostly it was a thing handed across the room to Ines, who then sent something out herself. The audience got the door secondhand, in the line.

So it didn't have to fool the valleys. It had to fool one woman four feet away, which was much harder.

"Give me a minute," she said. "Winston, talk to her about something."

She took the broken door and tried it with her eyes shut. Clack: the tongue hitting dead. The thump was still there, in a way; the pine frame still spoke when the little door met it. What was missing was the front. The tick. Up and over.

She tried a ballpoint, clicked just ahead. Too bright, a toy. She tried the cash drawer's catch, which was too long. She tried flicking the dead tongue with her thumbnail as she closed the door and got something tinny and late. Sol's cabinet had a cigar box of orphaned hardware and she went through it by ear, tapping things on the brass plate: a cupboard catch, a hasp, a skeleton key. The key was close. The key laid flat against the strike and tipped off it with one finger as the door came home gave a small bright step and then the wood.

"Try this," she said. "Don't look."

"I never look."

"From 'Monday.'"

"Not Tuesday, Oskar. Monday."

"Monday."

Shoes, boards, away. Key and door.

Ines waited. Everyone watched her wait. "Nearly," she said. "The thump's thin. It used to have a floor in it."

"The spring threw the tongue down. It hit harder." Petra looked around the table. She set the door frame on the square of linoleum, then took it off and set it on the footstep boards instead, the hollow box Sol had built for porches. "Again."

Key, door, and under it the box, answering.

Ines said, to the empty surgery, in the low unhurried voice, "Well. He won't come Monday."

It was the line as Petra had heard it on the air checks, the one that sounded like a woman alone, talking over her shoulder while she ran the tap. Winston grinned at the floor. In the window Dov had both hands raised like a man at a holdup.

"That's it?" Petra said.

"It's a quarter inch off," said Ines. "Do it exactly like that."

"I'm not sure I can do it exactly like that. It's three things with two hands."

"Then do it nearly like that every time, and I'll meet you." She put her glasses back on. "Sol was never exact either. He'd deny it."

They ran the scene twice more and the whole back half once, calf and all, and gave the room to the choir with a minute in hand. Petra taped the key to the door frame so it couldn't wander off and wrote on Sol's cue script, in a fourth color, *key flat on strike, tip w/ index, frame on porch box.* Then she put the two pieces of the spring in an envelope and wrote his name on it, because he would want them, and because he might know where there was another pantry.

On Thursday at seven fifty-one the light came on red, then her cue light, white. Oskar Thule's boots went away across the boards. Petra tipped the key and closed the door, and four feet off Ines drew a breath.
