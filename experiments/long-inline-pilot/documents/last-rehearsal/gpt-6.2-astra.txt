The Second Fare

On the first run, when Ada put a nickel through the fare box, it sounded as if somebody had dropped a shirt button into a cup.

“Thank you, sir,” said Ruth, who had to say it whatever the man had paid with. On the other side of the glass, Lewis drew a finger across his throat. The red bulb went dark. Nobody took off their coats. The room where WMMT did its plays had once been the chilled part of a butcher shop, and in February the heat came on according to a principle known only to the butcher.

“They’ll notice,” Ruth said.

“They’ll hear a nickel go in,” Ada said. “What else do they need?”

Ada was covering for her brother Mickey, who had caught his hand in a sash window the previous Sunday. He had left her four pages of instructions. For the streetcar he had put simply, *Use box*. She had done the conductor’s steps with half soles on a piece of oak, the door with a folding toast rack, and the passage over the switch at Mercy Street by running two egg whisks briefly against each other. Lewis had let all that go by.

“The little bell comes after,” Ruth said.

“I know what it used to do.”

“There’s a man at the other end of the line who waits up Thursdays to hear that sound.”

“Is there?”

“I lived with him thirty-one years.”

Lewis came in from the control room. He put his hand into the fare box up to the wrist, as if the little bell might have come off in there. He tipped it toward the light, then gave Ada back her nickel. “You’ve got until eight-fifteen. I promised the farm bureau we’d give them the room at half past. Their poultry man has to get home.”

The play was called *Exact Change*. Ruth played a conductor on the last car of the night. A young man rode with her to the end of the line in order to pay a fare he had beaten as a boy. The car went out of service in the morning. They had been rehearsing since six, and it had been evident since about six-oh-five that the poor fellow was going to confess something worse if given any encouragement. He was played by a high school English teacher named Bill who had brought a covered dish to be collected by his wife. Whenever he said, “I owe this city something,” there came up through the holes in the lid a distinct smell of cabbage.

Ada turned the box upside down over a folded towel. Three Canadian pennies fell out, and a brass upholstery tack. Farther up she found a lever no longer attached to anything. The nickel used to tip the lever, which threw a little hammer against a bell. The hammer was gone.

“Probably Mickey’s got it in his pants,” she said. Mickey brought things home in his pockets to solder after supper. When his first child was born, he’d had to hand over six screws and a bicycle valve before the nurse would let him hold her.

“Mickey fixes it after,” Ruth said. “How are you going to do it now?”

Ada looked at the clock. “You could let the man ride free.”

“He’s been riding free forty years. That’s the play.”

They tried the desk bell from reception, with Ada’s thumb laid against the dome to stop it ringing on. On the monitor it sounded like a sick person calling for assistance. They tried a teaspoon drawn along the teeth of a comb, which was Lewis’s contribution and did nothing for anybody. Then Bill found two small wrenches in the bottom drawer. He held one by a length of string while Ada struck it with the other. Ruth sat with her back to them. She said it was close.

“Close in what sense?” Ada asked.

“In the sense that I can tell a man is trying to make a bell.”

Bill put the wrenches back. “My wife can generally tell when I’m trying, too.” He checked the lid of the dish and returned to his mark, the white cross where he stood for his scenes inside the car.

Ruth was studying her script, a pencil stuck lengthwise into the waves above her ear. She had done a conductor’s run herself during the war. Ada knew it because she had seen a photograph in the station lobby, a row of women in uniform standing with their hands behind them, Ruth at the end with the point of somebody else’s elbow in her side. In the first scene she had corrected Bill about the way he got on. He was coming up too fast for the height of the step. Bill, who was six foot four, had obligingly got off again.

“Do you ever listen to this program at home?” Ada asked.

“I work here Thursdays.”

“I mean on a set. You want to hear what comes out of ours. You could fry an egg in the middle of a love scene and my mother would think they’d started kissing.”

Ruth took the pencil out of her hair. “You think people listen badly because they own cheap radios?”

“I think my mother owns a cheap radio.”

Through the glass Lewis held up five fingers, meaning the last five pages. They would come back to the opening. Ada got the car under way with a sewing machine motor under the table. At low speed the belt had an uneven pull that gave the car an agreeable load of passengers, though the only ones in the script were Bill and a drunk asleep behind him. On page nineteen the drunk woke up and got off. Ada took her foot a little off the pedal and the car went easier. She saw Ruth glance her way.

At the end of the line, Bill confessed to having watched another boy put off the car for the fare he himself had beaten. Rain in the gutters, running feet, the other boy’s face at the glass. Ruth let a second pass after his speech. The space she left was so exactly the space in which a conductor might decide she had been told worse things that Ada nearly missed the brake.

“Pay it, then,” Ruth said.

Ada dropped the nickel.

“That’s one,” Ruth said. “Now who’s going to pay for him?”

That was the last line. The script called for the little bell a second time, then music. Ada had to fish the nickel out again. While she was doing it, somebody in the corridor lifted the lid on Bill’s cabbage and replaced it very quietly.

Lewis came out. “You really need two nickels.”

“You really need a second customer,” Bill said. “Who pays the other fare?”

“I do,” Ruth said.

“On a conductor’s wages?”

“It’s my last day. I can go crazy.”

Ada set the nickel on the towel. “That’s why you want the bell. You don’t think they’ll know she paid.”

Ruth undid the top button on her coat at last. Underneath it she wore the blue wool uniform from the photograph. The shoulders had gone shiny and there was a neat blue insert under each arm. “I’d like them to know *somebody* did.”

Ada went out to reception for change. At the front desk sat a red-haired woman with a ledger open before her, writing down the names of listeners who wanted the poultry booklet. Already she had a page and a half, though the poultry man was still in the hall eating cabbage. At Ada’s request she opened the till. She gave her two nickels for a dime and asked what they were doing back there.

“Streetcar play.”

“Is Ruth in it?”

“Yes.”

“My dad used to take us down to ride her car. We could’ve walked wherever we went. We’d wait for hers.”

“What did the fare box sound like?”

The woman considered. “I don’t know. We were little. Dad paid.”

On her way back Ada stopped at the water fountain. From inside the studio she could hear Ruth saying her last line to Bill again, giving the weight to *him* this time. Outside the door it sounded mean. She said it once more, in a lower voice, and Ada heard, for the first time, the invitation in it. Bill apparently heard it too. There was a rattle of change, then Ruth laughed.

Ada went back in. “Let me do one with you,” she said.

She moved the box from the effects table to a chair beside Ruth’s microphone. She gave Bill a nickel and put the other in Ruth’s hand. Lewis, at the board again, made a turning motion for them to hurry up.

“From ‘Pay it, then,’” Ada said. “Do what you’d do if you had the money.”

Bill dropped his coin and Ruth asked who was going to pay for the boy. There was a small sound quite far down, then the second coin fell. Lewis kept the red light on through a good six seconds of wool rubbing against wool and Bill breathing through his nose. Then he played it back.

The small sound had been Ruth undoing the clasp of her change purse. She’d put the nickel away as soon as Ada had given it to her. Heard on the monitor, it was a dry double click, intimate as somebody taking out her teeth beside you in bed. After it came the blunt knock of the fare. You could hear whose money it was. You could hear what it cost her to have Bill stand and watch her get it out.

“Again,” Ruth said.

They went from the top. At the first fare Ada used the box on the chair and kept the motor running, so the nickel went in under the hum. Near the end she let the car run down until all you could hear was the belt making its last trip around. Bill told his story into the quiet. This time, when Ruth opened her purse, he turned his head a little from the microphone. It sounded as if he’d had the decency to look away.

At eight twenty-seven Lewis let in the poultry man. Bill took his empty dish home. Ada was winding the motor cord around her hand when Ruth came over with the fare box tucked under one arm.

“So would he have noticed?” Ada asked.

“My husband? Inside of a minute.”

“What would he have said?”

“Nothing till the music was over. Then I’d have heard about it.”

Ruth worked the little lever through the slot with the point of her pencil. It swung freely, making no sound. “I used to know who needed help getting on just by how long it took between the step and the bell. Some nights you could get most of your supper eaten up front in the dark.”

She put the box down. Ada slipped the two nickels into the pocket of the uniform for Thursday. From the studio came the poultry man’s voice, full and round and perfectly at home in all the kitchens of the county. He was telling people how to candle an egg. Hold it up, he said, and give your eyes time.

Ruth picked up her coat and the coins touched through the blue wool. Both women turned their heads.
