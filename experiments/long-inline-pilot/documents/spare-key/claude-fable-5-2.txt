# Blanks

The key came in a margarine tub with eleven others, and the woman carrying the tub set it on my counter the way you set down something you've been asked to carry for somebody else.

"These are from Okafor's," she said. "The dry cleaner. You'll have seen it's shut."

I had. The paper went up in the window in September, and by the end of October the rail that used to carry everybody's coats round the ceiling had been taken down and sold. My booth is in the arcade across the road, between the phone repair man and a unit that has been four different nail bars. I'd looked at Okafor's window for nineteen years. I'd had my one suit cleaned there twice, for funerals.

"I'm Ngozi," she said. "His daughter. I'm clearing the place. The landlord wants the keys back Friday, all of them, and I don't know which are his."

"Which are the landlord's."

"Which are anybody's." She tipped the tub slightly so I could see. "Dad's in Leicester with my brother now. He's not good on the phone. He says they're all important."

I took them out one at a time and laid them on the rubber mat. It isn't difficult, most of it. A key tells you what it is if you've handled enough of them. Two were for the shop's front door, a five-lever mortice, one original and one copy, and the copy was mine. I know my own cutting like handwriting. A cylinder key for the shutter padlock. A little flat one for a till. Two window keys, a meter cupboard key, the triangular kind the gas people use. A car key for a Vauxhall that I would bet had been scrap for a decade.

"That's the back door," I said, moving a rim cylinder key to the left. "Those go to the landlord. This lot's yours to bin or keep. He'll not want a till key."

She was writing it down on the back of an envelope, which I liked. Then I picked up the last one.

It was a Yale type, brass, worn pale along the shoulder where a thumb goes. No tag, no tape, no dab of nail varnish. But it was a copy, and it was one of mine. I use a particular blank for that profile, from a supplier in Walsall, with a round bow where most have a clover. And I deburr on the wheel in one pass, upward, which leaves a faint slant at the tip. I've been told it's a bad habit.

"This one isn't the shop's," I said.

"What is it, then?"

"Somebody's front door. A house or a flat."

"Dad's house?"

"Have you got his house key on you?"

She had. She put it beside the other and even she could see it: different cuts altogether, a high first peak where the unlabelled one ran low.

"So whose is it?"

I should have said I couldn't tell. It was nearly true. But I turned it over, and on the bow, in the place where the blank has its flat, somebody had scratched a small cross with a pin. I do that. I do it when I'm cutting a set for one customer and they want to know which is which. One plain, one crossed.

And I remembered the set, because it's the only time anyone has asked me to cut a key they were plainly embarrassed to be cutting. A tall woman, sixtyish, in the green tabard of the school kitchen. Four or five years back. She'd wanted one spare, then changed her mind while I was at the machine and wanted two, and when I asked did she want them marked she said, *Mark the one that's not for me.*

"I'd have to look into it," I said.

Ngozi put her pen down. "Look into it how? Is there a register?"

"No. There's me."

"Because if it's a customer's key," she said, "if somebody left it with him, I need to know. I've had three people come to the door about a duvet and a wedding dress. I've got a box of unclaimed things I'm legally supposed to hold for I don't know how long. I don't want a key in that box. I want to hand over a clean shop on Friday and get his deposit back, which is two months' rent, which he needs." She said it without heat. She had a lanyard round her neck from some office, turned so the photo faced her chest. She'd come in her lunch hour, I thought, and her lunch hour had been over a while.

"Leave it with me till five," I said. "The others you can take."

She looked at the key and then at me. "It's only a key."

"It's a key to where somebody lives."

She left it. She took the tub.

---

The woman in the green tabard was called Maureen Pask, and I knew that not from the cutting but from the arcade, the way you know everybody eventually. She came through on Thursdays with a wheeled bag. She lived in the maisonettes on Carrow Street, behind the dry cleaner's, close enough that from her kitchen she would have seen the steam coming out of his back vent.

I didn't have her number. I had her Thursday, and it was Thursday.

Before she came, I had Dennis. Dennis is the landlord's man, not the landlord. The landlord is a company with a name like a mattress. Dennis does the keys and the meter readings for nine units on the parade, and he brings me their work, which over a year is a tenth of my takings. He came at two for some padlocks I'd keyed alike and saw the brass on the mat.

"That one of Okafor's?"

"His daughter was in."

"She's supposed to be bringing those to me." He picked it up. Dennis has small, clean hands and he holds things as if pricing them. "What's this for?"

"Not the shop."

"You sure? Because there's a flat above. Was let separately, years back, then he had it for storage. There's a door on the side passage. I've got no key for that door on my board and I've had to tell the office that, and they've told me that if there's no full set Friday they're charging a lock change to the deposit. All locks. Three hundred and forty."

"It's not that door either. That's a mortice. I fitted it."

"You'd swear to that."

"I'd show you. Go and look at the door."

He put the key down, not on the mat but on the counter nearer himself. "All I'm saying is, if it came out of that shop, it's part of what comes back to us. Then it's on my board and it's not my problem what it opens. She hands me twelve keys, I tick twelve, he gets his money. She hands me eleven and a story, I've got to write the story down." He paid for the padlocks. "I'm trying to do her a favour, as it goes."

He was, by his lights. A key on Dennis's board sits on Dennis's board until the building falls down. Nobody would ever try it in a door. It would also, forever, be a key to Maureen Pask's home hanging in an office she'd never heard of, under the wrong label.

"She's back at five," I said.

"Then I'll be back at five."

---

Maureen came through at twenty to four with the wheeled bag. I'd been watching for the tabard, but of course she'd retired; she was in a blue anorak, and I nearly missed her.

"Mrs Pask."

She came over. She looked at the machine and the boards of blanks behind me, the hundreds of them hanging uncut, and not at the mat.

"I've had the keys in from Okafor's," I said. "There's one I think I cut for you."

She looked then. She didn't pick it up.

"He kept it," she said.

"It was in a tub with the shop keys."

"A tub." She almost laughed. "That'd be right. He had a tub for everything. Buttons out of pockets. He'd give you your buttons back in a little bag." She put one finger on the key and moved it a quarter inch. "I had a fall. That's all it was to start with. On the stairs, and I lay there from tea-time till the milk. After that my sister said give somebody a key, and I thought, who's there six days a week, seven till seven, right out the back? So I took him one. I said, if my kitchen blind's not up by nine, come and knock, and if I don't answer, come in."

"And did he?"

"He knocked twice in five years. Once I'd gone to Skegness and not told him." She did laugh then. "He was that cross. Stood on my step on the Monday in his apron and told me off."

"It's yours to take," I said. "I only wanted to be sure whose it was."

"What does the daughter want with it?"

"She wants to know it's not the landlord's. The landlord's man wants it to be the landlord's. Makes his count right."

"Does it cost her if it's not?"

"It might cost her an argument."

Maureen thought about that. She took her purse out of the wheeled bag and her own keys out of the purse, and held her door key against the one on the mat, plain bow by crossed bow, the same teeth. Hers was the bright one. His had gone pale.

"He never said he was going," she said. "I saw the paper in the window same as everybody. I thought, he'll come round with it. Then I thought, he's forgotten he's got it. I didn't like either." She put her own keys away. "Nobody's had my blind to look at since September. I've been putting it up anyway. Daft."

"You could give it to somebody else."

"I could." She didn't take it. "You hang on to it till the girl comes. I want her to have it from me. I want her to know what he did with it."

---

At five there were three of them at a counter built for one. Dennis had his board list. Ngozi still had her coat on and the envelope.

"It's this lady's front door," I said. "I cut it. That's my mark."

"Lent," Maureen said. "Not left. There's a difference, for your box."

Ngozi looked at her. "He had a key to your house?"

"He had the looking-out of my kitchen blind." And she told it again, shorter, with Skegness in. Ngozi listened with her mouth slightly open, and at the apron on the doorstep she put her hand over it.

"He never said a word."

"No, well. I asked him not to. I didn't want it round the parade that I'd been on the stairs all night."

Dennis cleared his throat. "I've still got twelve down from the inventory. Okafor signed for twelve in 2009."

"Then your twelfth's the Vauxhall," I said. "Or I'll cut you something for the hook."

He looked at me to see if I meant it. I took a blank off the board, a Walsall one, round bow, and held it out to him uncut. He didn't take it. But he wrote something on his list, a long something, and when he'd finished he said, "Eleven and a note," to Ngozi, and that he'd see her Friday, and went.

Maureen picked up the key at last. She held it out across the counter, the crossed bow first.

"You're in Leicester, are you?"

"No," Ngozi said. "I'm on Pell Road. I'm ten minutes."

"That's no good for a blind."

"No."

"Still," Maureen said, and kept her hand where it was.

Ngozi took the key. She put it on her own ring, working it round the split steel with her thumbnail, between her office fob and her father's house key.

I pulled the shutter half down after them. The blank was still in my hand, and I hung it back on its hook.
