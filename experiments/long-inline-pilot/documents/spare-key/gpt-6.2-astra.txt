The Working Copy

At a quarter past one, a boy came in for a copy of a front-door key and paid Sonia in fifty-cent pieces. He counted them out in stacks of four. His mother had begun work at the hospital and from now on he would be letting himself in.

“Try it while she’s home,” Sonia said.

“She won’t be home. That’s why I need it.”

He put the key on a blue shoelace and went out swinging it, already careless of the responsibility he had wanted her to understand.

Across the street, two men were taking the name off Bell’s Office Supply. The B came away whole. Each of the smaller letters broke at least once. Sonia had been looking at that sign from behind her counter for twenty-three years, and by the time the boy reached the corner she could no longer have said whether the apostrophe had been painted or made of metal.

June Bell came in holding a key on her flat palm.

“What does this open?”

It was a little brass key, dark at the shoulder, with a round head and a hole worn oval. Sonia picked it up.

“Some kind of cabinet, probably.”

“Could you come over and tell me which?”

“Is the question which cabinet, or what’s in it?”

“I know what’s in it. I need you to tell Walter it’s ours.”

Through the glass Sonia could see Walter Pike sitting on a red swivel chair on the pavement. He had worked for June’s father long enough to have acquired the settled bearing of a proprietor, though never the pay. Behind him stood a grey steel cabinet about the height of a washing machine.

“My father left everything in the shop to me,” June said. “Walter says I can have the cabinet but the contents belong to other people. He’s refusing to get off the chair.”

“He’d have to be sitting on the cabinet for that to work.”

“He has a bad hip. Come across, would you?”

Sonia turned her sign to BACK SHORTLY. Her lunch, still in its paper, went in the drawer she kept for customers’ umbrellas. The shop had no sensible place to put a sandwich, but plenty of places to put keys.

The cabinet had twelve shallow drawers and a single lock at the top. She recognised the key before she tried it. For years Bell’s had sent people over for duplicates. A copy for the new Saturday girl, a copy for the porter, a copy once for a man who came in with a live pigeon held to his chest. She remembered the bird because he had set it on the counter to get his money out and it had stayed exactly where he put it, as if terrible terms had been agreed upon elsewhere.

The key turned. Sonia pulled open the top drawer. Inside stood a row of khaki envelopes with names on them.

“Thank you,” June said.

“That’s the cabinet it fits,” Sonia said.

“And whose key was it?”

“Whoever had it, I suppose.”

Walter rolled his chair closer by hauling on the doorframe. “Show her one.”

June took out an envelope. From it she drew a photograph of four women on a beach. Three were sitting on a blanket, the fourth bending down to ease off a shoe. The fourth woman’s long hair hung over her face and almost reached the sand.

“People brought things in to have them copied,” June said. “Dad kept whatever they didn’t collect. I’ve rung everybody I could trace. The historical society will take the rest provided I sign for them. They’re coming at four.”

“She means sign them over,” Walter said.

“They have proper storage. They’ll put the pictures on their website and somebody might recognise a grandmother.”

“Somebody might recognise another man’s wife.”

“Walter has been enjoying that possibility since breakfast.”

He looked at the photograph. “Mrs Vacca,” he said, touching the bent woman’s bare ankle.

“You can’t even see her face,” June said.

“Her husband took the picture. He was particular about having all of her in the copy. She’d died by then.”

June put the photograph away. The men with the sign had gone to lunch, leaving the pavement gritty with plaster. A bus pulled in, let nobody off and took nobody on. Sonia could have crossed back to her sandwich. Instead she pulled out the second drawer.

The first envelope said MORRIS, E. That was her mother.

Inside lay a sheet of lined paper folded in thirds. At the top, her mother had written the address of the house where Sonia grew up. Beneath it were the words *Dear Sir, I am asking for the return of my deposit.* She knew the letter. Her mother had borrowed forty dollars from her at a time when Sonia, who was seventeen, was saving for a green leather jacket. The landlord had kept the damage deposit on their old flat, and until the money came back, her mother said, there would be forty dollars too little in the world.

Sonia had never got the jacket. She had not thought of it in years and could see now every silver tooth of the zip.

“Can I have this?” she asked.

“Of course,” June said. “Take the envelope too.”

Walter reached out. “Let me see the back.”

Sonia turned the page. It was blank.

“Hold it slant.”

There was a shallow dent in the paper at the bottom, the length of somebody’s thumbnail.

“That’s how he picked them up,” Walter said.

“Who?”

“Your dad,” he said to June. “Off the glass. His nail left that little moon. The ones with the dent went back to the customer.”

“Well, this one evidently didn’t.”

Walter took the letter and laid it on the cabinet. He rubbed his finger across the address and held it up for them. A little black dust had come away on the whorls.

“It’s a copy,” he said. “Even the dent’s a picture of a dent. We used to leave a sheet of grey card on top so the thin places wouldn’t come out black. Your father kept a copy of everything for a while. In case there was a complaint.”

June slit the next envelope with the key. Two small boys stood squarely in front of a car. She turned it over. The back was plain white.

“Photographic paper has a maker’s mark,” Walter said. “Usually.”

“You said these belonged to people.”

“I said the things in here weren’t ours to give away.”

She sat down on the doorstep. Someone had chalked the price of printer cartridges on the stone beside her left shoe. “Did he ask?”

Walter worked his chair back and forth over a loose bit of plaster until it was powder. “In those days it took a couple of goes to get a decent likeness. If you had one left over, you kept it. Nobody wanted to stand there and watch you put their kid in the bin.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

“I heard you, June.”

Sonia read the rest of her mother’s letter. There was a list of the things the landlord had claimed were damaged. A curtain track. A white enamel basin. Under that, in the careful upright hand her mother used with people who had the power to disappoint her, a sentence Sonia had never heard her say. *My daughter and I lived quietly in your house and took pleasure in it.*

How her mother would have hated having that read out in a room. Even this much, on a pavement, felt like something she ought to ask about. She folded it into quarters, then opened it and folded it again along the lines her mother had made.

“I rang your shop,” June said to Walter. “When Dad was in hospital. He wanted to talk to you about this cabinet.”

Walter’s shop was a counter at the Sunday market where he sold old postcards from revolving racks. Sonia had once bought a view of the municipal baths from him and sent it to her brother in Perth.

“I went in,” he said.

“You never told me.”

“He wanted me to take the top two drawers. Said there were some street scenes I could make a bit on.”

“And you came to tell me I shouldn’t give them away?”

“I came for my drawers. Then you told me what you were planning for the rest.”

Sonia’s shop door rattled across the street. The boy with the blue shoelace had come back. She raised her arm and he saw her. He held up the key.

“Didn’t work?” she called.

He shook his head. A woman in hospital greens stood beside him, holding a bicycle. Sonia went over and let them in. While she checked the cut, the boy explained that the key went in and wouldn’t turn. His mother took off her glasses and cleaned them on her sleeve. There was a small blue bruise where the bridge rested on her nose.

“You have to pull the door toward you,” she said at last.

“You never told me that.”

“I thought you’d seen me do it.”

“I’m always behind you.”

Sonia took a light burr off the key. “Try it with the door open first. Then shut it and try what your mum says.”

They went out together, the boy walking the bicycle so his mother could eat a pear. Sonia retrieved her sandwich. By then it was three o’clock. Across the road Walter and June were going through the drawers, laying envelopes in separate piles. She ate standing up. The sandwich was cheese and mustard, cut so badly on the diagonal that one half was nearly a whole sandwich and the other was a corner you might give a dog.

At ten to four June brought the little key back.

“We’ve put aside the ones we’re sure are originals,” she said. “Walter’s going to help me take them round. I told the society they can have the shop ledgers and the Christmas window photographs. They sounded quite pleased.”

“What about the copies?”

“Dad bought a shredder for the accountants next door in 1998. They paid for it but never could get it through their door. I’ve spent twenty-eight years telling him to get rid of it.”

Across the street Walter was feeding handfuls into a machine on wheels. It took a while on photographs. He stood with both hands resting on top of it as though waiting for a calf to drink.

“And his street scenes?” Sonia asked.

“He’s keeping the ones Dad took himself. There’s a lovely one of your place before you came. Used to be a shop that sold nothing but eggs. Did you know that?”

Sonia hadn’t known.

“Have you got a minute to make another of these?” June asked. “He’ll need to come and go till we’re finished.”

Sonia found a blank and set the two keys in the jaws of the machine. June leaned against the counter, looking through the window at the man across the road, who had taken off his jacket. There was a patch of fresh pink skin on his elbow. At some point that afternoon he must have scraped it. Sonia wondered which of them had found him a plaster and why it had come off.

She brushed the new key clean. June took it, then took the old one too, a key in each hand. For a moment she considered them. She put the bright one in her pocket.

At half past four, alone again, Sonia unfolded her mother’s letter and laid it on the counter. She tried to bring the old flat back entire. The front room came easily, then the passage and the narrow ledge where the telephone stood. For the kitchen she had only a square of sunlight on some brown tiles and the smell of a gas ring just turned off. She had forgotten living quietly there. It was a relief to have somebody’s word for it.

She put the letter back in its envelope. On the front, below her mother’s name, she wrote her own.
