At one fifteen, Mrs. Vale brought in a key wrapped in a receipt from the shop that had closed next door. The receipt was for two tins of tomatoes and a birthday candle in the shape of a seven. My father had sold that candle to somebody on his last Saturday in business. He had priced it at twenty cents, which was less than the tax on most things I sold.

“Tell me what it opens,” Mrs. Vale said.

I laid it on the counter. It was a small brass key with three cuts and a square head. Someone had ground the manufacturer’s name off the head, leaving a shallow dish in the metal.

“Where was it?”

“In the shop.”

“I got that far.”

“Under the scale. He told me to look under the scale.”

My father had kept a balance scale on the counter long after he stopped selling anything by weight. Children put their elbows on one pan and tried to lift a pound of iron with the other. During the clearance that morning I had sold it for twelve dollars to a man who said his wife would kill him. Mrs. Vale must have come in after I went back to my own shop. The door between the two was still unlocked.

“What else did he tell you?”

“That if I ever needed to get in, that was where the key would be.”

“Get in where?”

She took off her glasses and looked at me. Her glasses made her eyes look smaller, and without them she had the startled appearance of a person receiving news.

“I thought you’d know.”

My father was at my sister’s for the week, learning how to use a shower with a glass door. At noon he had called to ask whether I’d found a buyer for the scale. I could have called him back. I put the key on the rubber mat where I set things I meant to examine.

“What do you need to get into?”

“I need my rent money,” she said.

Mrs. Vale lived above the shop. For thirty years she had taken in alterations, using a bell on a string to summon customers from the pavement. Since the funeral parlor had started buying its suits online, she mostly hemmed trousers for boys with interviews. I knew because she sent them to me for shoe polish and I sent them back upstairs so she could tell them to take the polish off the welt.

She had given my father nine hundred dollars to hold. In her account, he offered after the plumbers came in March. They had left her kitchen door open and a man she didn’t know had stood there eating a pear. She had gone downstairs with her money that same evening. Each month she took some out and put some in. Her new landlord wanted the rent by five.

“Did he give you a receipt?”

“He used to count it in front of me.”

My brother Dan came through the connecting door holding a shelf bracket. He had bought the building from our father two years earlier, and that morning he had let us have the clearance without charging for another day. He meant to be generous about it. His new tenant was coming at four to take measurements for a dog wash.

“Whose rent?” he asked.

“Mine,” said Mrs. Vale.

Dan set the bracket on the mat, knocking the key to the floor. He went down on both knees to find it. The floor on my side was black rubber, and customers were always telling me how good it must be for my back.

When he stood up he held the key by the point.

“I’ve been looking for this,” he said.

He took it through to the shop. Mrs. Vale and I followed. With the shelves down you could see that my father had painted around them three times, so that the walls carried bands of cream, yellow and a blue so faint it looked like light coming through the plaster.

Dan crouched beside the old register. It sat on a stand with a steel cabinet underneath, which he planned to roll out onto the pavement. He put the key in the cabinet lock. It went all the way in. He tried turning it twice, with increasing faith.

“Let me,” I said.

He handed it over. I could feel one pin lift and catch. It was the right kind of blank. Somebody could have bought a dozen of them and cut keys for half the cabinets in the neighborhood.

“I can open this,” I said. “It’ll take a minute.”

Dan looked at Mrs. Vale. “What’s in it is Dad’s.”

“Some of what’s Dad’s is mine,” she said.

“I’ll ask him.”

He called from where he stood. We heard my sister answer and tell him that Dad had gone to sleep in the car on the way back from the pharmacy, and that she had left him there with the window down. Dan asked her to go wake him. She said she’d been out twice to check the shade and she was eating her lunch.

“Call back at three,” she said. “He’ll have to pee by then.”

Mrs. Vale sat down on the little wooden step my father had used to reach the cigarettes. She had a flat brown purse with what looked like sugar down one seam. She pinched a little of it out and tasted it. Then she pinched out some more and offered it to me.

“Peppermint,” she said.

Dan went outside to look for the dolly he had lent the man who bought the scale. I went back to my side and cut two copies of an apartment key for a woman whose daughter was coming home. She tried to decide between a blue cap and a red one, finally taking both. I never asked people whether their children were coming home for good. They tended to tell me in the pause while I brushed the filings off the keys. This woman watched me brush and said she had made up the bed.

At two I turned the sign to BACK IN TEN and brought my picks through. Mrs. Vale was standing at the counter, examining the round black circle where the scale had been.

“He did tell me where,” she said. “He said, ‘Put it with the money.’ I told him that was no place to put a key, and he said, ‘All right, the scale.’ So I knew if I had the key I’d be able to find the money.”

I opened the steel cabinet. It held a case of paper bags, three pink rolls of register tape and two white china cups. The cups had been upside down on their saucers long enough to leave clean rings. Inside one cup were the dead batteries my father used to give customers when they asked how big a battery they needed.

Mrs. Vale turned the other cup over. It was the kind that used to come free with a pound of coffee. She looked at its underside for a long time, and then set it back where it had been.

Dan returned with the dolly and a sandwich in a paper sleeve. He broke the sandwich in half, offered me the smaller half and gave the larger one to Mrs. Vale. She took it, picked out the peppers and handed them back to him.

“Could he have meant the money upstairs?” Dan asked.

Mrs. Vale stopped chewing.

“I mean his own money,” he said. “When Dad first opened, he used to go upstairs to make change. Mom kept a cashbox.”

“You’ve been in my apartment?” she asked.

“When I was nine.”

By then I knew the box he meant. I could remember my mother levering the tray up from one corner because she’d fill the space underneath with notes until the tray buckled. There was a keyhole just below the handle. The box itself had been green, though from a height it looked gray, like the money in it.

Mrs. Vale folded the sleeve around the uneaten end of her sandwich. “I have a green box,” she said. “Your mother lent it to me.”

She went up to get it. I heard the bell on her string ring once, as it did whenever she opened the door wide enough to admit herself sideways with both hands full. Dan wheeled the cabinet to the curb. A scrap of adhesive tape had stuck to the bottom. He peeled it off and carried it to my wastebasket, and for some reason I remembered him at nine, visiting every room of the house with a bowl of plum pits while my mother told him to put them in the garbage. He wanted somebody to see how many plums he’d eaten.

Mrs. Vale came down with the box wrapped in a dress. She laid it on the counter and put the dress over her arm. The box was greener than I remembered, especially around the handle, where the enamel was worn to a pale jade. There were scratches all around the keyhole.

“I tried my nail scissors,” she said. “Years ago.”

“What did Mom lend it to you for?” Dan asked.

“I needed something heavy to keep the pattern paper flat. She brought it up and then she couldn’t find the key. I told her it was doing its job.”

The key entered cleanly. I turned it a quarter turn and the bolt slid back. All three of us heard it. I left the lid down and pushed the box across to Mrs. Vale.

She had to put the dress down to open it. There was no tray inside. On the bottom lay a white bank envelope and, beside it, a loose brass handle from a drawer. Mrs. Vale pulled the envelope out and dipped her fingers in. She counted nine hundred dollars onto the counter, laying each hundred partly over the last. Some were made up of twenties. One was made up of fives.

Dan picked up the brass handle. “From the bread drawer,” he said.

“What bread drawer?” I asked.

“We had one.” He turned the handle over and ran his thumb across the two small threaded holes. “I remember the bread.”

At three the phone rang. It was my father, awake and delighted at having slept. He said my sister’s car was the first car in which he could get comfortable without moving the seat. Then he asked if Mrs. Vale had come in.

“She’s here,” I said. “We opened the box.”

He was quiet a little while. “Did she bring it down?”

“Yes.”

“I went up while she was at the dentist. I had her spare, for the pipes. I couldn’t think where else to put it once the shop was going.”

I watched Mrs. Vale gather the notes, trying to get all the edges even enough to go back in the envelope. She pushed too hard, and one of the fives folded. I thought about the man with the pear, whether he’d picked it up in her kitchen or come in eating it. I told my father I’d call him later.

“He used your spare to put it there,” I said.

Mrs. Vale finished putting away the fives. “I’d like my spare, too.”

I found it on the board next door, hanging from a nail with a paper tag giving her name and the best hour to catch her in. On the back my father had written *ring twice*. I brought it to her with the little brass key. She put her spare in the purse and slid the box toward Dan.

“Take it,” she said. “I’ve got an iron I can use.”

Dan rested a hand on the lid. Then he opened it and set the bread drawer handle inside. He asked me to cut a copy of the little key. He paid for it, even though he had heard me tell people all morning that I wasn’t charging for anything that had to do with the shop closing.

I cut the copy, and from the doorway he watched me work. Next door there was a knock, three brisk raps on a door that stood open. The woman from the dog wash called his name. He left the box on the counter between us and went to meet her with the bright new key in his hand.

Mrs. Vale picked up her dress. It was a man’s striped shirt with the sides opened, I saw now, and a skirt of the same material pinned to it. She folded it once over her forearm. I held out the old key, thinking she might like to keep it after all. She took it and fitted it into the lock. When she pressed the lid down, it lifted a little against her palm, and she had to press harder to turn the key. Then she set the key beside the box, where Dan could see it when he came back, and went upstairs to pay him.
