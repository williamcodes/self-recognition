# Board decision memo

**Decision requested this month.** Approve a six-month schedule that keeps Tuesday noon, Thursday evening, and Saturday morning, and adds eight Sunday morning sessions on alternate weeks during the first 16 weeks. Authorize up to **1,120 currency units** for the eight sessions. Keep the paid coordinator’s working week at 32 hours. After the eighth Sunday, return to the three regular openings for the balance of the six months unless the board separately approves and funds an extension.

The purpose of the trial is to find out how many people can use the shop because it opens on Sunday, and what happens to people who still cannot get there. The board should agree on the measures and decision rules this month, before it sees the first attendance figures. Members can then plan around a published calendar, and the board can judge the result against the reason it spent the money.

## Why keep the three regular openings

Last quarter the shop recorded 543 visits across the three opening times. Saturday morning accounted for 243, about 45 percent. Thursday evening accounted for 178, about 33 percent, and Tuesday noon accounted for 122, about 22 percent. Saturday has the highest recorded use and Thursday already provides a well-used evening option. Tuesday serves people who can come during a weekday. Removing it would put an established opportunity at risk before the cooperative knows whether those visitors could use another session.

These figures count visits. One person may account for several, and the totals give no count of people who wanted to visit while the doors were closed. The number of sessions actually held in each time slot was also not supplied. For the trial, the shop should record openings and cancellations so that the board can compare visits per session as well as totals. Keeping the existing pattern gives members continuity and makes changes in use easier to interpret.

## Why test Sunday first

Of 84 surveyed members, 35 wanted Sundays and 31 wanted weekday evenings. Members could select both. The four-response difference is too small to settle the choice, and adding the two counts would double-count anyone who selected both options. The survey also leaves a practical question about evenings. Some respondents may be able to use Thursday already, while others may need a different weekday. A second evening deserves consideration if the Sunday trial leaves that need unmet.

Sunday merits the first test because it offers a day the cooperative currently does not open, a substantial group expressed interest, and the board knows the session cost. Sunday morning is a scheduling choice for the pilot. Members were asked about the day, so the board should treat the morning start as an assumption to test. Use the same opening and closing times as Saturday morning to give members a familiar window. Ask people who request Sunday but cannot use the published hours what time they could attend. That will help distinguish a poor choice of hours from weak demand for the day.

The 12 partner referrals that arrived while the shop was closed establish a separate access problem. Their arrival times were not recorded. Any number from zero to 12 might have benefited from a Sunday opening. The cooperative should improve referral records across the whole week, including during the weeks between Sunday sessions. A referral log that covers only trial hours would conceal precisely the gaps the board needs to understand.

## Calendar, staffing, and spending authority

Schedule the first Sunday in the second week of the six-month period, then every other week through week 16. Each trial week uses four opening days, within the lease limit. All other weeks have the three regular openings. Before announcing the pilot, put the eight dates and exact hours on one calendar, alongside the regular schedule and a clear final Sunday date. Give the same calendar to partners, post it at the shop, and use it wherever members ordinarily check opening times. A list of dates is easier to act on than a notice that says “alternate Sundays.”

Assign four dates to each volunteer captain, alternating the lead role. Each captain would then lead one Sunday every four weeks, within the stated capacity of one extra shift per fortnight. That assignment covers leadership. Before publishing a date, the captain must also confirm the volunteers needed to run the shop under its usual staffing practice, including opening and closing. If the practice requires both captains, confirm both for each of the eight dates. That would use their full extra availability. Verify that the regular shifts remain covered in either case.

Eight sessions at 140 currency units each cost 1,120, leaving 80 of the 1,200 trial budget. A ninth would bring the cost to 1,260. The authorization should cover only the eight listed sessions, with actual expenditure recorded against each date. Captains should handle the Sunday tally and closing record as part of their shifts. Assign a board member to compile the figures and obtain the partners’ summaries. Confirm before launch that any coordination required fits within the coordinator’s existing 32 hours. If coverage falls through, notify members and partners promptly and record the cancellation. The coordinator should have no obligation to fill the gap.

## How to tell whether access improves

**Count people who gained a usable opening.** At each Sunday visit, offer one short, optional question: “If the shop had been closed today, could you have come to Tuesday noon, Thursday evening, or Saturday morning for this repair within the next two weeks?” Record yes, no, unsure, or no answer. Two weeks covers a full cycle of the trial. Count a visitor answering “no” as reporting an access gain. A “yes” can still indicate a valued convenience. Keeping that answer visible will let the board see how much of Sunday use came from people who already had a workable option.

Count each person reporting a gain once across the pilot. A simple way is to ask a second question after a “no” answer: “Have we already counted you on an earlier Sunday as someone who couldn’t come at another time?” Record repeat and uncertain responses separately. This produces an estimate based on visitors’ own answers without requiring names. Report the counts of unanswered and uncertain responses alongside the estimate. Also record whether the visitor received repair assistance or had to leave without being helped. A person who found an opening but could not get help reveals a different problem from a person who could not get through the door.

**Track use across the whole schedule.** For every opening, record the date, time slot, visits, and any cancellation or unusual disruption. At review, show weekly visits across all days, visits per held session for each time slot, and separate results for weeks with and without a Sunday. Compare the regular sessions with last quarter where the records support a like-for-like comparison. A drop on Saturday alongside a busy Sunday may suggest that people moved their visits. Some of those people may still have gained access, particularly if they could not attend Saturday that fortnight. Read the attendance pattern together with the visitor answers. Seasonal changes and differences between weeks limit what the totals alone can establish.

**Follow referrals through to an outcome.** Ask partners to record the day and time a person tried to use the shop, if known, and whether the shop was open. Record the time the partner sent the referral separately if it differs. Using an agreed reference without a name in the board report, record whether the person received assistance within two weeks, was still waiting, or had an unknown outcome. Include the total number of referrals so the board can interpret the number arriving during closures. Continue the log throughout all six months. The closing weeks will show which gaps persist once the Sunday trial ends. The earlier 12 cases supply context, but their missing times and the unknown total referral volume prevent a reliable before-and-after rate.

## Review and decision rules

After the fourth scheduled Sunday, the designated board member should check the records with the captains and partners. Confirm that members can find the dates, shifts have been covered, spending is on track, and the access question is being asked consistently. Correct problems while there are still four sessions left. If an opening is cancelled or the tally is incomplete, retain that fact in the report. Four poorly advertised sessions would tell the board as much about how it announced the trial as about members’ ability to attend.

For this pilot, adopt **16 distinct visitors who report gaining access and receive repair assistance** as the threshold for considering a Sunday extension. That is an average of two people newly able to get help per planned session. At the full authorized spend, meeting the threshold would cost 70 currency units per such person. This is a proposed board judgment about what the trial should achieve, rather than an estimate derived from the survey. Report the actual spend and actual count together. If nobody meets the definition, report zero people helped on this measure and omit the cost-per-person calculation.

At the first board meeting after the eighth scheduled Sunday, consider an extension if the trial meets the threshold, regular openings remained staffed, and the coordinator’s hours held at 32. Review outstanding referrals and people turned away alongside the headline count. Before authorizing more dates, identify the funding and confirm a sustainable roster. If the trial falls short despite reliable delivery and good response coverage, keep the three regular openings and use the recorded days and times to assess a different weekday evening or a different Sunday window. If cancellations or missing responses leave the result inconclusive, record that uncertainty. The calendar still reverts to three days, and another trial would require its own proposal.

At six months, the board should receive a one-page closeout showing sessions planned and held, actual spending, the number of distinct visitors who gained access and received assistance, and the timing and outcomes of partner referrals. Include the times most often requested by people who still could not attend. This gives the board a basis for its next schedule decision and gives members a concrete account of what the eight extra openings achieved.
