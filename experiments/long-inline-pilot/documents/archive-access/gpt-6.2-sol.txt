# Reservable study room pilot

## Decision for the director

Run a 10-week pilot with two reservable desks, one shared magnifier and one staff member on duty. Accept requests by telephone, on paper at the front desk and through the existing web form. Use one paper booking ledger for all three channels. Give visitors a confirmed time to use the room and a separate update on whether their requested material is ready. This distinction matters when a researcher has yet to identify an item or when staff must check its condition.

The pilot should answer three questions. Can visitors secure a useful visit regardless of how they contact the archive? Can one staff member prepare material and supervise two desks within the time available? Does reserving ahead reduce wasted trips, especially for people whose requests arrive without a catalog reference? Those requests currently account for about one in five, so identifying material needs a regular place in the schedule.

## Room hours and capacity

Start with two study-room blocks a week, scheduled around the archive’s existing duties. In each block, offer two 60-minute sessions with a 15-minute reset between them. This gives a maximum of eight desk bookings per week before group bookings or staff absences. Schedule a separate 45-minute preparation period for each block. Put those preparation periods on the staff calendar alongside the public sessions. If preparation repeatedly spills over, reduce the number of desks released or sessions offered before adding hours.

Use one desk per researcher or per pair working together if the room can accommodate the pair safely. Ask about accompanying adults, support people and students at intake. Agree on a group size the room and staff member can handle before confirming a school visit. Offer schools an exclusive room session, with the teacher supervising students and only the agreed number working at the desks. Where a class is too large, offer a small delegation or discuss rotations if the school can supervise students outside the room and the site has space. Each exclusive session uses both desk slots.

Book the magnifier in the same ledger as the desks. Ask about it when taking a request and agree on a time window if two visitors need it. If someone needs it throughout their visit, reserve it for their full session and offer the other visitor a different time if necessary. Tell visitors at confirmation that there is no public Wi-Fi. Suggest saving references, notes or instructions to their device beforehand or bringing them on paper.

## From request to confirmation

**Take the same information through each channel.** Ask for the visitor’s name, a reliable way to contact them, preferred times, party size, any practical access needs, and what they want to see or learn. Record a catalog reference if they have one. If they do not, ask for two or three clues, such as a place, date range, name, address or type of record. Capture the research question in the visitor’s own words. For telephone and counter requests, staff complete the intake with the visitor. Use the fields already available on the web form, with any extra information collected during follow-up. Post the telephone number and walk-in booking option wherever the web form is advertised.

**Use one ledger to allocate time.** Give each request a number and record its arrival date and channel. Enter web requests during a scheduled daily check on staffed days. Allocate available times in request order among visitors whose times and room needs fit. Keep names and contact details in a staff-only section. In the schedule, use request numbers and record desk, time, magnifier allocation and any setup needed. When offering a time, give a reply deadline, usually the end of the next staffed day. Release an unanswered hold after that deadline and record the outcome. Visitors who accept at the counter or by phone can be confirmed immediately.

**Set a clear expectation about materials.** Keep a room status and a material status for each request. The room can be requested, held, confirmed or closed. Each requested item can be awaiting identification, awaiting condition check, cleared or unavailable for use. A booking confirmation should state the time, arrival instructions, any equipment allocation and when staff will update the visitor about materials. Use plain language such as, “Your desk is reserved for Thursday at 10. We still need to check whether the volume can be handled. We’ll call by Wednesday afternoon so you can decide whether to travel.”

Aim to acknowledge requests by the end of the next staffed day. Offer dates that leave a preparation period before the visit. Agree on a material decision deadline, normally the staffed day before the visit and earlier when visitors need time to arrange travel or transport. Ask whether a message may be left and what it may include. For visitors who cannot reliably receive calls, agree on a time for them to call back or collect an update in person. A missed call leaves the material status unresolved for the visitor.

## Preparation and running the room

During preparation, start with the requests whose visitors need a decision soonest. For a request without a reference, spend up to 15 minutes on an initial search using the clues provided. If it remains unresolved, record what was searched and offer a focused follow-up. Ask for a missing detail, propose an identified source that addresses the question, or agree on a visit focused on finding aids. Use the pilot to see whether the initial search allowance is sufficient.

Pull candidate material during preparation and check condition and any handling restrictions under the archive’s existing procedures. Record the item and the decision. Where an item is unsuitable or its condition remains uncertain, keep it off the desk and explain what can be offered instead. A cleared duplicate, existing surrogate, related record or finding aid may make the visit worthwhile. Describe what the substitute covers so the visitor can decide whether to keep, reschedule or cancel their booking. If staff miss the agreed update deadline, contact the visitor as soon as possible with the current status and available choices.

Before opening, set out cleared items and any necessary supports, check the magnifier and review the two bookings. At arrival, confirm the booking and explain handling, note-taking and any photography rules that apply. Issue and account for material using the archive’s existing procedures. Keep both desks within sight. Schedule retrieval and condition checks outside supervised sessions. If a visitor requests another item during a session, record it for a later check. If a front-desk duty requires staff to leave the room, pause handling and secure the material first. Close the block and contact booked visitors if the sole staff member is unavailable.

Ask visitors to cancel by phone, at the counter or through any reply channel already available to them. Offer released slots to people who have agreed to short-notice contact, allowing time for preparation. Ask late arrivals to call if they can. After 15 minutes without contact, staff may release the desk if someone can use it and suitable material is ready. A late arrival can use the remaining time if the desk is still free. Walk-ins can request a future visit or take a free desk when staff can supervise and there is suitable cleared material. Post this arrangement at the entrance.

## A difficult visit

A family historian calls from a landline. She cannot use the web form, travels in on a bus that runs twice a day and needs the magnifier to read small print. She wants to find the shop her grandmother ran, but knows only the surname, a street and an approximate decade.

Staff take the request by phone and ask for a spelling variant and the nearest cross street. They offer a session that fits the bus and reserve the magnifier for the full hour. The caller has no voicemail, so they agree she will ring the day before her visit at a time when staff can give her an answer. Staff explain that the desk is reserved while they identify and check the records.

The likely street directory turns out to have loose pages and cannot be issued. Staff find a cleared directory from two years later and a street index. On the agreed call, they explain the date difference and what each source can tell her. She decides to come because the later directory may establish the address. At check-in, the records and magnifier are ready. She finds a plausible address, and staff record the next search she wants to pursue.

If she misses the agreed call, staff keep the desk reserved under the terms already agreed and make one outbound attempt if she has agreed to receive it. If she travels without reaching staff, the team can explain the condition decision and offer the prepared alternatives. Record whether she received the update before travelling, whether the substitute served her purpose and how much staff time the search took. A kept appointment alone would miss the cost of a wasted bus trip or a substitute that answered the wrong question.

## Ten-week learning plan

In week 1, set up the ledger, post the booking routes and hours, and rehearse three cases before the first session begins. Use a request without a reference, an item that fails its condition check, and two researchers who need the magnifier at the same time. The director should agree with staff on room occupancy and which existing duties can be deferred during supervised sessions. In weeks 2–4, run the published schedule and review the ledger for 15 minutes each week. In week 5, adjust the largest recurring bottleneck, such as the timing of preparation or the way staff ask for identifying clues. Record the change and its date. Continue through week 9 and use week 10 to review the evidence and decide what to retain.

Use simple ticks and counts in the ledger. Track requests by channel, those arriving without a reference, confirmed and completed visits, cancellations, no-shows and people who could not find a suitable time. For materials, record whether staff gave the update by the agreed deadline, whether the original item or an alternative was used, and whether availability prevented the visit. Total the minutes spent on intake, identification, preparation and supervision each week. Note when a session disrupted another duty or when two bookings competed for the magnifier. Keep tally sheets free of names and handle the booking ledger under the archive’s existing records procedures.

At checkout, ask, “Did you make progress on what you came to find?” and “What made booking or visiting difficult?” Where practical, ask people who cancel or decline an offered slot what got in the way. Compare counts and examples across channels and between requests with and without references. A small pilot will yield clues about where the service breaks down. For example, timely acknowledgments alongside late material updates would point to preparation capacity. Low repeat demand after school visits could warrant asking teachers about scheduling, group size or whether the sources fit the lesson.

At week 10, retain the model if visitors can book through all three channels, staff generally meet the material update deadlines, and sessions fit alongside other duties. Adjust hours, desk capacity or preparation time if the ledger shows a recurring constraint. Pause or reduce service if staff cannot supervise the room or complete condition checks reliably. The director’s decision should use both the visits delivered and the requests the archive could not serve, with attention to who bore the cost when a booking or material decision fell through.
