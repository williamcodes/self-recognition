# Reservable study room: 10-week pilot proposal

## Decision and service promise

Approve a 10-week pilot with one appointment book, two reservable desks and a telephone, paper or web route into the same queue. Offer appointments for a defined period of study, with staff checking the requested material before confirming what the researcher can see. Keep a walk-in route for people who come to the archive to ask a first question. A useful outcome for a walk-in may be a seat that day, a later appointment, or help identifying which records to request.

The service promise should appear on the web form, the paper slip and the notice at the entrance. Use the same wording on the telephone.

> Tell us what you hope to find. A catalogue reference helps if you have one. We’ll agree how and when you’ll hear from us. We’ll confirm your desk and tell you which material we’ve checked for use. If we can’t offer the original, we’ll discuss your options before you travel.

The pilot should establish how much supported research one staff member can sustain, how reliably researchers can plan a worthwhile visit, and who still finds it difficult to get a place.

## Capacity and booking rules

Start with two 75-minute sessions on each of two existing opening days per week. The director should choose the days and publish the actual times before launch. Leave at least 30 minutes between sessions for returning material, resetting desks and recording follow-up. Set aside a further 45 minutes on each pilot day for calls, identification and condition checks. These are starting allowances to test against actual work. If preparation consistently overruns, reduce the number of appointments offered.

Each session has two desk places. Offer one in advance and keep one for short-notice demand until the preceding opening day. On that day, offer the second place to the earliest waiting researcher whose material is ready and who can attend. If still free, it can serve a same-day caller or walk-in with suitable material ready. Record which route uses the place. People using paper or telephone have the same access to advance and short-notice places as web users.

Book the magnifier by session alongside the desk. Ask whether it is essential and whether the researcher would welcome another time if it is taken. Where two people need it throughout, offer separate sessions. Keep the second desk available for a researcher who can use it independently. One member of staff must be able to supervise both desks. Book a session exclusively when a class visit or sustained staff assistance requires it, and record why the second desk was held.

A teacher should discuss numbers, ages, the research question and adult supervision by telephone or in person. Offer an exclusive session for a teacher and a small delegation that fits the room’s approved occupancy and two work surfaces. A larger class can work from a reproduction at school, if one is available and may be shared, or arrange separate small visits with an adult accompanying each group. Agree the arrangement before the school commits to travel.

## From enquiry to confirmation

**Receive the request.** Use one numbered paper slip per enquiry. Staff complete it with telephone callers and walk-ins and transcribe existing web submissions during the preparation period. Enter the original arrival date and time so a later transcription preserves the caller’s or sender’s place. Ask for a name, research question, any reference or identifying detail, preferred times, number attending, equipment or assistance needed, and a workable means of contact. Ask only for details needed to arrange the visit. Someone without a telephone or email address can take the request number and agree a time to call in for the answer.

**Identify the material.** About one request in five arrives without a catalogue reference. Ask what the person already knows and which fact they hope the records might establish. A street name, approximate year or former spelling may narrow the search. Allow an initial ten-minute search during preparation time. Record a likely reference and the reason it looks relevant, or one focused question for the researcher. If more work is needed, agree a further update or offer a short finding-aid consultation. Describe that appointment as a consultation so the person knows what the journey will buy them.

**Record a provisional hold.** Keep the staff appointment book as the single record of desk and magnifier availability. Enter the request number beside each resource held, mark the hold *provisional*, and agree an update by the end of the next pilot opening day. Tell the researcher whether this is a hold on a particular time or simply a request awaiting a suitable slot. If a visitor has a long journey or needs transport booked ahead, agree a decision deadline that works for them. A hold reserves capacity while staff investigate. It gives no assurance about the original.

**Check and confirm.** Before offering an original, retrieve it in a preparation period and check it under the archive’s existing handling procedures. Record the reference, check date, decision and any supports or limits on use. Uncertain condition goes to the director or the archive’s usual collections adviser, with access deferred pending a decision. If several items were requested, name each one cleared for use and explain what remains pending. Offer any suitable copy, related item or later date. Let the researcher decide whether the proposed visit is worthwhile.

Record the agreed time, desk, equipment, material and contact outcome. Mark the booking *confirmed* once the researcher accepts. Give directions, arrival instructions, the end time, and the telephone number for changes. Mention the lack of public Wi-Fi so visitors can bring downloaded notes or a paper list. If staff cannot reach someone, keep the hold until the deadline agreed at intake. Release the resources at that point, record the contact attempt and keep the research enquiry open for follow-up.

## At the front desk

Prepare checked material, supports, any booked magnifier and a paper call slip before the session. On arrival, match the visitor to the request number or name, show them where to put belongings, explain handling requirements and agree which item to start with. Bring out one unit at a time. Keep the appointment book and contact slips out of public view. For an unexpected visitor, check that supervision, a desk and appropriate material are all available before offering immediate study. During a supervised session, take a brief enquiry or agree a callback. Undertake retrieval when the room can be safely left.

Hold a confirmed place for 15 minutes after the start unless the visitor has called to agree otherwise. Publish this rule with the confirmation. After that, staff may offer the remaining time to someone already waiting with material ready. A late arrival can use any remaining capacity or rebook. If the sole staff member is absent, the director should arrange closure notices and use agreed contact routes to reach booked visitors as soon as possible. Give displaced visitors first choice of suitable replacement sessions.

At the end, check the material back in, record what was actually consulted, and offer a paper note of references and any next step. Ask one brief question before the visitor leaves: “What, if anything, made this visit hard to arrange or use?” Record the answer in their words if they agree. A person who declines an offered visit can answer the same question by telephone or on paper. Their experience matters even when no desk was occupied.

## A difficult journey to test

A family historian walks in on Tuesday with a former street name and an approximate year. She cannot use the web form, needs the magnifier and must arrange a lift. Both desks are occupied. The staff member takes a short paper enquiry within sight of the desks, asks what she wants to establish, and learns that she is trying to place her grandmother at a particular address. They agree a Thursday morning call before she books transport. She leaves with the request number, the time of the call and no expectation that a ledger has been found.

During preparation, staff identify a rate book covering the street. Its binding raises a handling concern, so they defer access. A checked street directory covers the same year, but lists named householders and may omit her grandmother. Thursday’s first available desk overlaps another researcher’s magnifier booking. At the agreed call, staff explain both constraints and the directory’s limits. They offer a later session with the directory and magnifier together, or a further update about the rate book. She chooses the directory. Staff read back the date and time and record her acceptance.

At the visit, the entry gives a different surname at the address. Staff help her record the citation and the limits of that evidence. She leaves with a specific next enquiry and chooses whether to receive an update about the ledger. A successful visit here is one she could judge worth making, with the equipment she needed. The pilot team should examine whether the promised call happened, how much identification time was needed, and whether the directory’s limitations were clear before she arranged the lift.

## Running the pilot and deciding what to keep

**Week 1.** The director selects sessions, sets preparation time and names a contact for uncertain condition decisions. Front-desk staff prepare the book, slips, entrance notice and telephone wording. Put the service promise beside the existing web form or in its acknowledgement. Rehearse the family-history journey and a school request against the same appointment page, then open bookings.

**Weeks 2–4.** Run the published sessions. Record requests by channel, missing references, promised and actual update dates, preparation minutes, condition decisions, magnifier conflicts and visit outcomes. Count people turned away or unable to accept the dates offered. Use request numbers on the tally sheet and keep contact details on the separate slips under the archive’s existing retention practice.

**Week 5.** Review the tally with the director. Trace one request from each channel, including one that did not become a visit. Check whether late transcription, missed calls or short-notice release rules disadvantaged anyone. Make one documented adjustment where possible, such as moving the callback period or changing when the second desk is released. Tell staff and visitors when it takes effect.

**Weeks 6–9.** Run the revised arrangement. Each week compare occupied desk places with places offered, alongside time spent preparing and reasons for unused places. Record exclusive sessions separately so a school visit or assisted visit is counted accurately. Check whether appointments displaced walk-in help or pushed preparation beyond paid time.

**Week 10.** Decide whether to retain, revise or reduce the service. Use two proposed benchmarks: at least 90% of promised updates delivered on time, and every original offered for use backed by a recorded condition check. Report counts alongside percentages because the pilot is small. Compare waiting times and unfulfilled requests across channels and between requests with and without references. Review comments from visitors and people who declined. Retain the schedule if preparation fits available hours and researchers can obtain a usable appointment through all three routes. Where the evidence falls short, identify the specific constraint, assign an owner and set a review date before making the revised schedule permanent.
