# Decision paper | Instrument loans for the coming season

## Decision requested

Approve a one-year no-deposit trial, a six-week preparation plan, and a review after the first term. Authorize the loan coordinator to allocate playable instruments under the access rules below. Ask teachers to identify teaching needs and acceptable instrument substitutions, and ask the treasurer to oversee repair spending. Subject to written confirmation of eligible costs, accept the sponsor’s offer of 3,000 currency units for one year. The sponsor bars spending on deposits.

The recommendation rests on two judgments. First, a refundable deposit requires a family to find cash before a player can take an instrument home. Second, the orchestra has practical ways to improve returns and arrange repairs whichever deposit policy it chooses. A trial lets the board test whether those measures are sufficient while keeping entry open to families who would struggle to pay upfront. The board should retain the option to reconsider deposits after it has evidence about missed returns, their causes, and their cost.

## What can be promised before rehearsals

Of 75 instruments, 62 are recorded as out on loan. That leaves 13 outside recorded loans, against 14 applicants waiting. Even if all 13 are playable, suitable, and available, there is one fewer instrument than waiting applicants if each needs a separate loan. Instrument type and size matter. A spare full-size violin does little for a child who needs a quarter-size instrument.

Nine instruments are due for repair. The figures do not show how many of those nine are among the 13, how many are still with borrowers, or which can safely be played until their scheduled repair. If all nine are among the 13 and must be withdrawn immediately, only four would be available before repairs, assuming the other four are playable. If some are with borrowers, repairs could interrupt current players’ access. Sibling sharing adds another uncertainty because the number of instruments on loan does not establish the number of children already served.

The first commitment to families should therefore be a date for an individual update. A place on the waiting list should become a confirmed offer only when the coordinator has identified a suitable instrument, checked its condition, and established a handover date.

## Access decisions

**Upfront payment.** A refundable deposit gives a family a financial reason to complete a return and may reassure volunteers who worry about unrecovered instruments. Its practical value depends on the amount, the refund timetable, and the circumstances in which the orchestra could retain it. A deposit also ties up household cash for the duration of the loan. A waiver could preserve access, although it would require families to know it exists and feel comfortable asking for it. Finance volunteers would have to record each payment as money owed back to the family, reconcile balances, and handle disputed deductions. Sponsor funds cannot pay or underwrite deposits.

The six late returns last term give the board a reason to improve recovery. They do not establish how long families were late, whether another player missed a rehearsal, or whether a deposit would have changed the outcome. The four families with outdated contact details may overlap with the late returns, but the extent of any overlap is unknown. On this evidence, the board has a stronger basis for fixing contact and return arrangements than for setting a particular deposit amount.

**Who receives the next suitable instrument.** Apply one published rule to new applications and renewals. Give priority to a player who would otherwise be unable to participate because they lack regular access to a suitable instrument. Accept a short statement from the family. Among players with equal need for the same type and size, use the date of the original request. Where dates are missing or tied, use a documented draw. Give current borrowers enough notice to plan for a return if their loan will end. Invite families to flag an incorrect size, date, or access assessment before handover.

**When siblings can share.** Keep sharing voluntary. Ask whether both children can practise and attend their scheduled sessions with one instrument and whether the size and setup suit both. If sharing works, list both players under the same instrument number, name one adult contact, and agree one return date. If schedules clash or the instrument fits only one child, assess the second child under the ordinary allocation rule. A household should be able to explain that sharing has stopped working without losing its existing place in the queue. Record each child served so the board can see what sharing actually achieves.

## Recovery decisions

**Make each loan traceable.** Use a single register with an entry for each instrument, including its identifying number, type, size, condition, location, named players, responsible adult, checkout date, and due date. At handover, the coordinator and family should confirm the condition and accessories together. Give the family a copy of the agreement with the return arrangements and a direct way to report damage or request an extension. Keep contact details available only to volunteers who need them for loan administration.

Ask the adult to confirm their preferred contact method at checkout and renewal, and send a short confirmation while there is still time to correct an error. Offer a paper or telephone route for families who use email infrequently. An alternative contact can help if the family wishes to provide one. Updating a telephone number should take one message or a conversation at rehearsal.

**Make returns easy to complete.** Send a reminder one week before the due date. Offer a staffed handback at rehearsal and a second appointment for families who cannot attend. Record the return immediately and give a receipt covering the instrument and accessories. If an appointment is missed, contact the family within two working days to agree a new date or document an extension. After a week without agreement, the coordinator should refer the case to the program lead for personal follow-up. When a delay affects a waiting player, give that family a revised update date without disclosing the borrower’s circumstances.

## Repair decisions and budget

At the average bill of 90 currency units, nine repairs would cost an estimated **810**, or 27% of the sponsorship. If all nine bills equal the average and qualify under the agreement, **2,190** would remain. The average is a planning figure. It gives no assurance about the cost or completion date of any individual job.

Authorize an initial repair allocation of up to 810 in total, once the treasurer confirms eligible spending and when funds will arrive. Obtain itemized estimates and turnaround dates before committing work. If the total exceeds 810, the treasurer should bring the revised amount and its effect on available instruments to the board for prompt approval. Hold the estimated balance for eligible costs during the year until the initial repair bills and the sponsor’s terms are clear. Exclude refundable deposits from spending plans and from income forecasts.

A teacher should flag playability concerns, and a qualified repairer should assess uncertain cases. Withdraw any instrument judged unsafe. In scheduling work, give priority to a repair that prevents a current player from losing access or releases a suitable instrument for a waiting player before rehearsals. Where two jobs would serve equally urgent needs, use quoted completion dates and costs to decide the order. Record the fault, approval, bill, and date each instrument returns to service. Encourage families to report faults promptly, and explain that ordinary wear is covered by the program. Any proposed charge for damage should receive individual review, with the family able to respond.

## Six weeks to opening rehearsal

| Timing | Action and responsibility |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | The coordinator reconciles the register against instruments in storage and contacts current borrowers. Teachers identify the instruments due for repair and any immediate playability concerns. The treasurer confirms sponsor terms and payment timing. |
| Week 2 | The coordinator checks contact details, sibling arrangements, renewal requests, and each waiting applicant’s type and size requirements. The repairer provides estimates. The treasurer authorizes eligible work within the approved allocation. |
| Week 3 | The coordinator applies the published priorities, sends confirmed offers where possible, and gives every remaining family an individual update. Teachers check proposed substitutions with the player before an offer is finalized. |
| Week 4 | Volunteers arrange returns and fittings. The coordinator checks repair progress and contacts affected families if a completion date slips. The program lead reviews unresolved shortages by type and size. |
| Week 5 | Volunteers complete handovers, record condition and accessories, and issue agreements. The coordinator follows up on unaccepted offers and gives families a clear response deadline before reallocating an instrument. |
| Week 6 | Teachers verify at the first rehearsal that each issued instrument fits and plays properly. The coordinator records who is still waiting, the reason, and the next date each family will hear from the program. |

## How the board should judge the trial

At the first-term review, report access in terms of children as well as instruments. Show how many of the original 14 applicants received a suitable instrument by the first rehearsal, how many took up a later offer, and how many still lacked one. Report new applicants separately so later demand does not obscure the opening result. Give the reasons for unfilled requests, including size mismatch, repair delay, and lack of stock. Count children sharing successfully and record cases where sharing proved impractical.

For recovery, report the number of loans due, the number returned late, the length of delays, and any instrument still outstanding. Distinguish agreed extensions from missed dates. Compare the late count with last term’s six, while recognizing that a rate comparison requires the number due in each term. For repairs, show actual spending, time out of service, and how many completed jobs put an instrument into a player’s hands. The treasurer should report sponsor funds received, spent, committed, and available.

Continue the trial through the year if difficulties can be addressed through scheduling, contact updates, or repair capacity. Bring the policy back earlier if missed returns repeatedly prevent other children from playing or if unrecovered losses threaten the program budget. Before proposing a deposit, identify the specific problem it could address, the amount families would need to provide, the waiver and refund arrangements, and the likely administrative cost. At year end, the board can then decide both whether to retain no-deposit access and how to fund repairs when the sponsorship ends.
