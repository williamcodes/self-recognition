# Instrument loan program decision paper

## Decision requested

Approve a one-year, no-deposit trial for instrument loans, starting this term. Accept the sponsor’s 3,000 currency units, subject to written confirmation of eligible expenses, and authorize repair assessments immediately. Adopt a published allocation policy and a single loan register that records sibling sharing, verified contact details, instrument condition, and return dates. Review readiness two weeks before rehearsals and review trial results at the end of each term.

The board has two distinct decisions to make. **Access policy determines which young musicians can borrow an instrument and what they must do before receiving it.** It includes deposits, waiting-list priorities, and sibling sharing. **Recovery and repair policy determines how the orchestra keeps track of instruments, gets them back on time, and returns them to playable condition.** It includes the loan register, reminders, follow-up, condition checks, and repair funding. A refundable deposit sits across both decisions. It creates an upfront requirement for families and may provide money against a charge if the agreement permits a deduction. The proposed trial removes the upfront payment while strengthening the practices that address the problems recorded last term.

## What the orchestra can plan for today

The orchestra owns 75 instruments and has 62 recorded as out on loan. That leaves 13 recorded as off loan, with 14 applicants waiting and nine instruments due for repair. The figures do not establish how many playable instruments can be offered. The repair list may include instruments still with borrowers, and the physical count may differ from the register. If all nine repairs are among the 13 off-loan instruments, only four of those 13 would be available before repairs, assuming those four are playable. If all 13 off-loan instruments are playable, they could cover at most 13 of the 14 applicants before any further returns. In either case, instrument type, size, and suitability may reduce the number of placements.

Poorly recorded sibling sharing adds another uncertainty. The count of 62 should represent individual instruments, while the register should also show every musician using each one. A family may have one instrument serving two players, and an arrangement that worked last term may no longer work if their rehearsal schedules change. The first inventory check should establish where each instrument is, who uses it, whether it is playable, and when it could next be issued.

Last term, six instruments came back late and four families had outdated contact details. The number of loans due for return is unknown, and the two groups may overlap. These counts identify two processes to improve. The orchestra should confirm contact information before issuing or renewing a loan and give families clear return dates and practical ways to meet them. It should also record how many loans are due so the board can compare late-return rates across terms.

## Access choices

A **refundable deposit** gives the orchestra funds it can hold temporarily and potentially apply against an agreed charge. Its value depends on the deposit amount, the circumstances in which deductions are permitted, and the consistency of collection and refund procedures. Even when fully refundable, it requires families to have money available at the point of borrowing. A lower amount, installments, or a confidential waiver can reduce that burden. Each adds an administrative step, and a waiver requires families to ask for an exception. If the board adopts deposits, it should approve the amount, waiver process, deduction and appeal rules, and refund timetable before applying them. Finance volunteers would need to track each family’s balance as money potentially owed back. The sponsor’s funds cannot be spent on deposits.

A **one-year no-deposit trial** lets eligible families accept a loan without an upfront payment and gives the board a full cycle of issue, renewal, and return to assess the program. Its financial trade-off is that the orchestra holds no deposit to draw on when a borrower owes an agreed charge. For the trial, use a signed agreement, an issue and return condition record, and a clear process for dealing with damage or loss. Keep ordinary wear and routine servicing within the program budget. For damage beyond ordinary wear or for loss, contact the family, review the circumstances and the documented condition, and use the orchestra’s approved terms before assessing any charge. Give families a way to ask for a review or hardship consideration. Apply the trial terms to new loans and renewals, and honor any refund terms already agreed with existing borrowers.

Allocate available instruments by teaching suitability and need. A teacher should confirm the instrument type and size each player can use. Among applicants who can use the same instrument, give priority to those without reliable access to a suitable instrument for rehearsals and practice, then use application date to break ties. Apply the same criteria to renewal requests at the scheduled review point, while honoring current loan terms. Tell families how offers are made, when they need to respond, and when they can expect another update. Give an applicant who is passed over because an instrument is unsuitable their original application date for the next suitable offer.

Keep sibling sharing available where the teacher and family confirm that both musicians can use the instrument safely and have sufficient rehearsal and practice time. Record one accountable adult, both musicians, and the agreed schedule against the same asset. Review the arrangement at renewal or when rehearsal schedules change. If sharing no longer meets one sibling’s needs, assess that player for a separate loan under the standard criteria.

## Recovery and repair choices

Assign one loan coordinator to maintain the register and a named backup to cover absences. Use one record per instrument with its asset ID, type and size, location, condition, loan status, accountable adult, registered players, verified contact information, and due date. Record repair status and expected completion dates in the same register. Limit family contact details to the people who administer loans. Teachers need access to availability, suitability, and sharing information for their pupils.

At issue or renewal, ask the responsible adult to confirm a preferred contact method, record the return or review date, and provide a copy of the agreement. Send a reminder one week before the due date. If the instrument is overdue, follow up directly within two working days and offer a practical handover time. Log any agreed extension and notify the teacher if the instrument was expected for another player. Refer unresolved cases to a designated board member after a week overdue so that further contact and any action under the agreement have a clear owner. Record receipt and inspect the instrument promptly so it can be offered again or sent for repair.

At the stated average of 90 currency units, the nine known repairs would cost about **810**, leaving **2,190** of the sponsor’s **3,000** if all nine repairs are covered and actual costs match the estimate. The 810 is a planning figure. Obtain estimates and completion dates, including any parts delays, before committing the money. Authorize the treasurer and loan coordinator to approve up to 1,000 in aggregate for the initial repairs once the sponsor confirms eligibility. If estimates exceed that amount, bring the quotes and proposed repair order to the board promptly. Keep the balance available for approved repair and maintenance during the year, subject to the sponsor’s terms. Track commitments and invoices against the grant and report the remaining balance each term.

Prioritize repairs that restore a suitable instrument for a waiting player or prevent an interruption for a current player. Use turnaround time to decide between jobs of similar urgency. For a costly repair, ask the teacher and treasurer to assess likely useful life and whether the instrument can serve an identified need before approving the work. Classify instruments with playability or safety issues as unavailable until cleared. Confirm what the grant will cover this year and use actual spending to estimate the repair budget the orchestra will need once the sponsorship ends.

## Six-week implementation

**Week 1.** Appoint the coordinator, backup, and board escalation contact. Reconcile the register with instruments in storage, loan agreements, and the repair list. Confirm the sponsor’s terms and obtain repair estimates with dates. Ask all 14 waiting applicants to confirm the instrument and size they need, whether they have another suitable instrument, and how to reach them. Confirm contact details with current borrowers whose loans are due for renewal or return.

**Weeks 2 and 3.** Send priority repairs for service and confirm which loans are continuing, ending, or shared. Ask teachers to check suitability and timetable conflicts. Make offers as soon as playable instruments and agreed handover dates are confirmed. Give each family a response deadline and contact them directly before reallocating an unanswered offer where contact difficulties are apparent.

**Week 4.** Give the board an instrument-by-instrument readiness report for remaining demand. Show confirmed placements, repairs expected before rehearsals, returns still outstanding, and unmet needs by instrument and size. If gaps remain, ask teachers to identify workable temporary arrangements, such as a supervised instrument for rehearsals or a compatible loan from a partner organization where one is available. Confirm any cost and funding source before committing. Tell affected families what they can expect for the first rehearsal and when they will next hear from the coordinator.

**Weeks 5 and 6.** Inspect repaired and returned instruments, complete fittings and handovers, and verify that each issued loan and sharing arrangement is recorded. Follow up on any remaining gaps before the first rehearsal. Keep unplaced applicants on the list with their original application dates and a named contact for updates.

## Review and proposed resolution

At the end of each term, report how many applicants received a suitable instrument by the first rehearsal, how many are still waiting by instrument and size, and how many declined an offer and why, where families choose to say. Report the number of loans due and the number returned late, how long they were overdue, unresolved loans, and contact problems. Show confirmed sibling-sharing arrangements, repair turnaround, repair costs, and grant balance. Ask families and teachers whether sharing, communication, and return arrangements worked in practice. Use aggregate figures for board reporting and keep family details in the loan register.

At year-end, review the deposit choice against both access and recovery outcomes. If delays cluster around missed reminders or difficult handovers, the board can address those causes directly. If significant loss or damage persists despite reliable records and follow-up, quantify the cost and assess how much a proposed deposit could realistically recover, what it would cost to administer, and how it would affect families’ ability to borrow. Use the experience of this trial and the known limits of last term’s records when judging changes.

**Proposed resolution.** Approve the one-year no-deposit trial, the allocation and sibling-sharing rules, and the loan administration procedures in this paper. Accept the 3,000 sponsorship subject to written confirmation of eligible spending, authorize up to 1,000 for initial eligible repairs, and require a readiness report two weeks before rehearsals, termly reports, and a year-end decision on the loan policy and repair budget.
